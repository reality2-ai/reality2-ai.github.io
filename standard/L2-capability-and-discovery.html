Reality2 Standard — Layer 2: Capability and discovery

Status: Draft — this names the editorial maturity of the text, and nothing else. It is not a licence to deviate: every requirement here is normative now and the implementing lanes build from it (d041, Roy: standard is the canon). The only text open to replacement is a value marked PROVISIONAL(...) at its own clause. (Subject stated because a status field is scoped to a question the reader may not be asking, and a claim with an unstated subject will be given the subject the reader brought — composer, 2026-08-03.) Date: 2026-08-04

Verbal forms. shall = requirement · should = recommendation · may = permission · can = possibility and capability.

Introduction

Layer 1 answers one question about a transmission it overhears: is this ours? Layer 2 answers the next two: who is out there, and what can they do?

It answers them per bearer, and it answers them without connecting to anything. A hive announces itself by broadcasting, repeatedly, on every bearer it has. Anything in range hears it. Nothing is negotiated, nothing is agreed, and the announcement is addressed to no one.

That shape is chosen, and two properties follow from it.

A hive that does not announce itself does not exist. There is no directory, no registry, and nothing to ask. Peers find each other by hearing each other, so a hive silent on a bearer has removed itself from that bearer's network — not degraded, absent. Nothing reports this, because from outside there is no difference between a hive that is silent and a hive that is not there. Clause 4 therefore makes announcing obligatory rather than sensible, and states no role, mode or activity that suspends it.

An announcement is a signpost, not a passport. It says something is here, and it is this kind of thing. It does not say whose it is, and Clause 5 forbids it saying so. A stranger within range learns that a hive exists and what class it belongs to; that is public by design, because a network you cannot discover is a network you cannot join. What a hive belongs to, and what it will do for you, are settled above the trust boundary — and a stranger who has completed every filter in this document still cannot make anything happen.

Layer 2 does not choose anything. It produces the picture — which peers are present, on which bearers, how recently, of what class, with what capabilities. Choosing which bearer carries a given frame is Layer 3's, and it is made from this picture together with the bearer profiles of Layer 1. The layer that gathers evidence and the layer that acts on it are kept apart so that the evidence stays usable for decisions nobody has thought of yet.

1 Scope

This document specifies how a hive announces its presence, its class and its capabilities, and how another hive discovers them.

It specifies the obligation to announce, on which bearers, and what an announcement contains and does not contain.

It specifies the progressive filtering by which a discovering hive spends effort only on candidates worth it.

It specifies how announcement cadence relates to the fade behaviour declared at Layer 1.

It specifies what Layer 2 provides to the layers above.

It establishes what conformance at this layer consists of, and identifies what is not decided.

It does not specify the byte layout of an announcement on any particular bearer, which belongs to that bearer's binding document. It does not specify which bearer carries a given frame, which is Layer 3, and it does not specify membership, authorisation or anything that happens above the trust boundary.

3 Terms and definitions

For the purposes of this document, the terms and definitions given in the documents listed in Clause 2 apply, together with the following.

3.1 beacon

broadcast announcement of a hive's presence, class and optionally its capabilities, addressed to no one and requiring no response

3.2 beacon identifier

opaque identifier carried in a beacon, stable for the emitting hive and derived from no other identifier the hive has

Note 1 to entry: Opaque is the load-bearing word: the identifier is a random value, carrying nothing an observer can decode and nothing derivable from the hive's identity, its device identity, or any trust group. It is a handle, never a credential and never a derivation.

3.3 class

(defined in 01-terminology.md 3.9)

3.4 class hash

fixed-width hash of a class, carried in a beacon where the class itself would not fit

3.5 capability summary

compact representation of the events a hive publicly handles, admitting false positives and no false negatives

3.6 scanner

hive receiving and evaluating the beacons of others

3.7 build mode

identity of the image a device was flashed with: development, whose internals may be exposed for inspection, or production, whose internals are not exposable because the exposing code is absent from the image

Note 1 to entry: A build mode is which code was flashed. It is never a setting — there is no switch, and changing mode means flashing the other image.

3.8 sighting

record that a named peer was heard on a named bearer at a known time

3.9 duty class

statement by a hive of whether it is continuously receptive or receptive only at intervals

Note 1 to entry: The duty class states, hive to hive, the same fact a platform declares as its availability at Layer 0 (P8). The Layer 0 declaration is published text, read by a person; the duty class is the on-air form of it, carried as 5.4b requires. Layer 3 is what acts on it — the choice between flooding for a destination and holding a frame until it wakes.

4 Announcing is not optional

4.1 Every hive, every bearer in service

4.1.1 A hive shall emit a beacon on every bearer in service.

4.1.2 A hive shall not emit a beacon on a bearer that is absent or disabled.

4.1.3 A hive shall receive and evaluate beacons on every bearer in service.

4.1.4 A duty-cycled hive satisfies 4.1.1 and 4.1.3 by beaconing and receiving while it is receptive, and shall declare its availability as Layer 0 requires.

4.1.5 A hive shall not declare itself duty-cycled in order to reduce its beaconing below what 6.1 or 6.2 requires of it while receptive.

Note 1 to entry: 4.1.1 is stated of every hive, with no exception for size, role or purpose. Beaconing is a consequence of being on the network, not a feature added to a hive that wants to be found.

Note 2 to entry: 4.1.2 is what makes the obligation satisfiable. In service means the hive provides the bearer and has not disabled it, so a hive with no radio beacons on the bearers it does have, and a hive whose operator has disabled a bearer does not announce on it. A hive announces itself exactly where it can be reached.

Note 3 to entry: A bearer in service can be temporarily unable to send. The obligation is to beacon on it, not to beacon at a moment when it cannot. A bearer reporting itself unable carries no beacon until it can, and that is not a suspension under 4.2.

Note 4 to entry: 4.1.4 and 4.1.5 are two halves of one rule. A hive that sleeps cannot announce itself while asleep, and requiring otherwise would make every duty-cycled hive non-conforming by construction. What 4.1.5 prevents is the same fact being used as a reason — a hive is not excused from announcing because it could sleep, only while it actually is, and its declared intervals are what tell a scanner the difference.

Verification: enumerate the hive's bearers in service and observe a beacon on each while the hive is receptive. A bearer carrying traffic but no beacon is non-conforming.

4.2 Nothing suspends it

4.2.1 No role, connection state, election outcome or activity shall suspend beacon emission or reception on any bearer in service.

4.2.2 Being asleep under a declared duty cycle (4.1.4), and a bearer being temporarily unable to send, are not suspensions under 4.2.1.

Note 1 to entry: This is stated because the opposite is a natural implementation. A hive opening a connection, or acting as one side of some negotiation, appears to have no reason to keep announcing itself. It has one: everything else in range is still looking for it, and it does not know who.

Note 2 to entry: A hive that stops announcing while busy is discoverable only when idle, which inverts what a discovering peer needs. The failure is silent and it is intermittent — the worst combination to diagnose, because the hive is present, working, and invisible only sometimes.

4.3 A liveness message is not a beacon

4.3.1 A hive shall not substitute any other periodic message for a required beacon.

4.3.2 A scanner shall not infer a beacon's content from any other message.

Note 1 to entry: The two are easily confused because both are periodic and both indicate a hive is alive. They differ in audience and in content: a beacon is addressed to nobody, carries a class, and is meant to be heard by hives that know nothing about the emitter; a liveness message is exchanged between hives that already know each other and carries no class. Accepting one for the other leaves a hive invisible to everyone except those who already found it.

4.4 What a beacon is

4.4.1 A beacon shall be unauthenticated.

4.4.2 A beacon shall carry the minimal declarations of Clause 5 — and, where the bearer has room, the optional capability summary of Clause 8 — and nothing else. A receiver shall treat a beacon as evidence of presence and of the declarations it carries, and of nothing more: never of identity or membership, and a carried capability summary is a reason to ask, never an answer (8.3). Information beyond the beacon's carriage shall be obtained by asking (7.2.2, 8.3); the ask path is authoritative.

Note 1 to entry: A beacon is trivial to forge, and this is accepted rather than mitigated. Nothing is granted on the strength of one. It causes a scanner to consider a candidate and nothing else; every consequential act is gated above the trust boundary, where forgery is not cheap.

Note 2 to entry: The beacon carries minimal information; if more is required, it is requested. (Ruled 2026-08-01.) The declarations that ride in the beacon are exactly the ones the ask path cannot supply: the build-mode declaration gates every cooperative exchange (5.4a.5), so it must precede any ask; the duty-class declaration says when a peer can be asked at all. Everything else is a request away, and the answer to a request — not the beacon — is the authoritative form of it.

5 What a beacon carries

5.1 A beacon shall carry:

a) the bearer's discovery marker, as required by Layer 1;

b) a beacon identifier (3.2); and

c) a class hash (3.4).

5.2 A beacon may carry a capability summary (3.5). A beacon emitted by a development device shall carry the build-mode declaration (5.4a); a beacon emitted by a production device shall not. A beacon emitted by a hive receptive only at intervals shall carry the duty-class declaration (5.4b).

5.3 A beacon shall not carry:

a) any identifier of, or value derived from, the hive's trust group; or

b) any identifier of the hive other than the beacon identifier — not its hive identifier, not its device identity, and no value derived from either.

Note 1 to entry: 5.3 b) is checkable, and the check is the point: given the hive's other identifiers, none of them — hashed, truncated, or transformed — reproduces any part of the beacon identifier. A beacon identifier that embeds another identifier fails silently: it looks opaque, decodes to nothing, and hands a passive observer the durable identity the trust boundary exists to keep off the air.

Note 2 to entry: 5.3 a) is what makes the beacon plane usable by everyone. A beacon announces presence to all and identity only to those who can resolve it, so the same broadcast serves discovery by strangers and recognition by members without carrying two different things.

Verification: compute the candidate derivations — the hive identifier, the device identity, each trust-group identifier, and their hashes — and compare against the beacon identifier. No match, whole or part. A repeat-suppression counter, where a binding document provides one, is permitted: the beacon identifier is stable, so a counter links nothing that was not already linked.

5.4 The beacon identifier

5.4.1 A beacon identifier shall be generated randomly by the hive, or installed with its identity under Layer 0, and shall be independent of every other identifier the hive has.

5.4.2 A beacon identifier shall be stable: a hive shall not change it in normal operation. The one event that replaces it is re-persona (Layer 5, 5.1.4), because the new persona is, observably, a different hive — an identifier that survived the change would link the two.

5.4.3 A hive holding information about a peer may associate the peer's beacon identifier with that information, and shall obtain the association above the trust boundary — never by decoding the identifier.

Note 1 to entry: What a stranger gets from a stable beacon identifier is stated in Clause 11 and is accepted deliberately, in place of a rotating identifier this standard's earlier drafts carried. The rotation defended one narrow case — a device that beacons and never transmits data, observed across time by a passive stranger — at the price of a shared absolute-time dependency on exactly the platforms least able to hold one. Where that case matters, the mitigation is operational, not cryptographic: an observer's cost is physical presence, and the privacy position of this standard is refusing aggregation, not churning identifiers. (Settled by ruling; the ruling record is editing history and not part of this standard.)

Note 2 to entry: 5.4.3 is the resolution model, and it is a table lookup. Members learn the association between a beacon identifier and a hive when they learn everything else about the peer — above the trust boundary, at provisioning or on verified contact. The identifier itself teaches nothing to anyone.

5.4a The build-mode declaration

5.4a.1 A hive running a development image shall declare so in every beacon it emits, on every bearer.

5.4a.2 A hive running a production image shall not emit the declaration. A production beacon shall carry no development-related bytes.

5.4a.1a A hive that cannot determine the build mode of the image it is running shall declare development.

Note 0 to entry: 5.4a.4 fails closed on the scanner side and nothing covered the emitter. A scanner that cannot read the field treats the mode as unknown; a hive that cannot determine its own mode had no rule at all, and the pleasant answer — say nothing, which reads as production — is the one an implementer reaches for. The failure directions are asymmetric: a development device that fails to declare is admitted to a production network (5.4a.5, Layer 5, 9.2), a relaxed-trust device inside a production boundary; the reverse costs the device its admission and is loud. 01-terminology.md 5A.2 states the general form — assert the value that grants least — and this is its instance. Absence of certainty is not absence of development. (Added 2026-08-02, PROVISIONAL(supervisor), finding SS205; the gap was found by the core lane. Note that under 5.4a Note 1's construction this condition should not arise — only a development image carries the declaring code — so a hive reaching it has already lost that structural property and should be read accordingly.)

5.4a.3 A scanner reading a development value shall treat the hive as a development device.

5.4a.3a A hive running a development image shall not place in service a bearer whose binding document states that its announcement cannot carry the declaration (Layer 1, 11.1 j)).

Note 0a to entry: The production-value branch of 5.4a.3 was unreachable and has been removed. It let a scanner read a production value where the bearer's announcement layout carries one — but 5.4a.2 forbids a production hive to emit the declaration at all, so no conforming production beacon could ever supply that value, and Layer 1, 11.1 j) assigns bytes only to the development declaration. A branch no conforming emitter can trigger is not a permission; it is a reader waiting for something that will never arrive, and an implementer building it would conclude that absence must mean something else. What remains is the asymmetry the design intends: development declares, production is silent, and 5.4a.4 governs what silence may be read as.

Note 0b to entry: 5.4a.3a closes what a stated inability leaves open. Layer 1, 11.1 j) lets a binding document say its announcement cannot carry the declaration — honest, and 01-terminology.md 4.5's form — but saying so does not satisfy 5.4a.1, which requires the declaration on every bearer. Without 5.4a.3a a conformant binding would be unusable by every development hive while appearing available, and a development hive using it would be non-conformant on that bearer with no clause saying so. The bearer is not forbidden; it is forbidden to development hives, which is the narrowest rule that keeps 5.4a.1 universal.

Note 0c to entry: 5.4a.3a takes the bearer out of service rather than silencing it, and the first drafting got that wrong. Clause 4.1 requires a hive to emit and receive beacons on every bearer in service, and nothing suspends that obligation — so do not announce on this bearer asked a development hive to be non-conformant to 4.1 in order to be conformant to 5.4a.1, which is not a choice a standard may hand an implementer. A bearer that cannot carry the declaration is not in service for a development image, and 4.1 then has nothing to require of it. (Corrected 2026-08-02, PROVISIONAL(supervisor), finding SS212, raised by an independent review of the fix landed an hour earlier.) (Both added 2026-08-02, PROVISIONAL(supervisor), finding SS210, raised by an independent review.)

5.4a.4 A scanner unable to read the field — absent, truncated, or unreachable — shall treat the hive's build mode as unknown, and shall not present unknown as production or as secure, to a person or to any trust decision.

5.4a.5 (Revoked 2026-08-04 by Roy's ruling, d231. It required a scanner to refuse a cooperative exchange with a peer whose declared build mode differed from its own; that requirement no longer applies. The clause number is retained rather than reused, because it is cited from three places in this document and from a requirements register row.)

5.4a.5a A hive shall participate in the transient network irrespective of its own build mode or any peer's, and shall not refuse discovery, neighbour formation, relaying or delivery on the ground of a declared build mode.

5.4a.5b An implementation shall assume that any party within range of a bearer may be recording traffic and metadata, whatever build mode it declares or fails to declare, and shall not treat a declared build mode as evidence that a party is not doing so.

Note 0b to entry: The bound is in range, not peer, and the widening is the whole point. A carrier relays; a peer forms a neighbour relation; a silent listener does neither — and it is the only one of the three that no clause reached. Observing does not require relaying, and a silent listener declares nothing, joins nothing, and is refused by nothing. (This clause first said any peer, which is a proper subset of anyone in range and therefore bounded the wrong set — corrected 2026-08-04 on android's measurement.)

Note 0 to entry: The revocation's reason is enforceability, and it is Roy's: a device can say it is prod, but still report on traffic … it is easy to get around and impossible to police. An unenforceable control is worse than no control, because implementers rely on it — 5.4a.5 invited a hive to treat a production declaration as assurance against observation, and nothing ever made that true. 5.4a.5b replaces a partition that could not hold with an assumption that always does, which is the only form of this protection a mesh can actually offer. Build mode is now a property of the persona a device uses, not a partition of the network (Layer 5, 9.2 governs the persona and is unaffected). (Added 2026-08-04, PROVISIONAL(standard) as to wording only; the ruling is ratified as d231.)

Note 0a to entry: This is not a relaxation of the trust boundary and grants nothing. Note 2 below already states the operative half — nothing is granted on build mode alone, because trust still requires membership and a valid signature, established above the trust boundary — and that sentence is now the whole of the mechanism rather than half of it. What changes is that a device no longer refuses to carry or discover; what does not change is what a frame may do once carried, which the delivery gate decides and 5.4a.5 never touched.

Note 1 to entry: The asymmetry between 5.4a.1 and 5.4a.2 is the design. Only a development image contains the code that writes the declaration, so a production device cannot falsely declare development by construction — and a production beacon spends no bytes on a field whose absence carries the information. What the asymmetry costs is 5.4a.4: absence has more than one cause — a production emitter, a truncated announcement, a decoder that dropped the longer development form whole — and a scanner cannot tell which. Reading every absence as production would manufacture a safety claim nobody made, for exactly the frames that lost the field.

Note 2 to entry: The declaration is unauthenticated, deliberately, because its forgery is conservative by construction. Falsely declaring development makes the claimant less trusted, harming only itself. Falsely claiming production gains nothing, because nothing is granted on build mode alone — trust still requires membership and a valid signature, established above the trust boundary. The one forgery that would pay — claiming production by omission — is exactly what 5.4a.4 closes, by refusing to let absence assert anything. A reviewer noticing the field is unsigned and proposing to sign it is adding a mechanism the threat model does not need.

Note 3 to entry: (Superseded 2026-08-04 by d231, and retained because the argument it makes is the one the ruling answers.) It read that development and production devices do not work together, because a development device implies an ability to track metadata, and that one development device inside a production network is a metadata tap on it. The observation is correct and the conclusion drawn from it was not: a production image that chooses to record traffic declares production and records it anyway, so the boundary could be crossed by anyone willing to cross it and was load-bearing only for those who would not. 5.4a.5b keeps the observation and drops the boundary — every peer is assumed to be that tap, which is true whatever anyone declares.

Note 4 to entry: (Superseded 2026-08-04 by d231, and this is a correction rather than a deletion — the note framed as the one exception a case that is now the ordinary one.) It read that a development hive may carry a production network's traffic only where it is the only connector, that the exception is to carriage and never to membership or cooperation, and that where any same-mode path exists it is preferred and the exception does not apply. The middle clause survives and is now the whole of the rule: carriage never implies membership or cooperation, and the frames carried are ciphertext protected end-to-end above the trust boundary. What falls is the preference and the exceptionality — under 5.4a.5a, devices of every build mode carry for one another as a matter of course, and no same-mode path is preferred over any other. What the note conceded is what 11.4 now states generally: exactly the metadata any party in range sees.

Note 5 to entry: The declaration is a class attribute, identical across every development device. It carries no per-device identity, so it does not conflict with 5.3. It does partition the anonymity set into development and production — accepted, because development devices are bench equipment whose visibility is the point.

*Verification: present a scanner with a beacon declaring development and with a beacon truncated before the field. It reports development and unknown. It cannot be asked to report production, and the earlier procedure that required it was wrong twice over (finding SS215): 5.4a.2 forbids a conforming production hive to emit any declaration, so no such input can be constructed — and a conforming production beacon and a truncated one are the same observation, which 5.4a.4 answers with unknown by design. Scanner operation is shown by the development case, which is the only verdict a conforming emitter can produce — and it is the vacuity guard too: a scanner reporting unknown for the declaring beacon has merely stopped reporting. The sentence that stood here named a readable production value as the guard, contradicting the sentence before it — the third correction to this one paragraph, each removing a dependency on a value 5.4a.2 forbids anyone to emit. A retraction is not finished when the clause changes; it is finished when every sentence resting on the retracted thing has been found.

5.4b The duty-class declaration

5.4b.1 A hive receptive only at intervals shall declare its duty class (3.9) in every beacon it emits, on every bearer, together with the longest interval between its receptive windows, coarsely stated.

5.4b.2 A continuously receptive hive need not carry the declaration, and may carry a continuous value where the bearer's announcement layout provides one.

5.4b.3 A scanner reading an intermittent value shall record the peer's duty class and its stated interval as part of the sighting, and shall provide them upward as Clause 9 requires.

5.4b.4 A scanner unable to read the field — absent, truncated, or unreachable — shall not treat the absence as a claim of either class. The peer's duty class is unknown.

Note 1 to entry: The field is carried by the hives it protects, and by nobody else. A hive that sleeps is the one that loses when peers transmit at its silent radio: every copy is spent, none is heard, and on a regulated bearer the budget it cost is the budget the hive's next waking needed. Declaring costs that hive a few bytes and buys its neighbours the ability to hold a frame rather than flood it — the choice Layer 3 makes from this value. A continuously receptive hive spends nothing, and a declaration lost in transit costs nobody anything but the optimisation: an unheard claim leaves the peer's duty class unknown, which is exactly what it was before the field existed.

Note 2 to entry: The declaration is unauthenticated, deliberately, because its forgery is conservative in both directions — the same test the build-mode declaration passed (5.4a Note 2). Claiming to be intermittent delays only the claimant's own inbound traffic; claiming to be continuous, or staying silent, costs a flood already bounded by hop limit and replication budget. Neither lie gains an attacker anything from anyone else. That is what licenses beacon carriage: a value whose forgery harms only the forger does not need a verified message to travel — and requiring one had left the value unobtainable by exactly the hives the shared mesh asks to relay for groups they do not belong to.

Note 3 to entry: The interval is stated coarsely because the beacon carries what a stranger's hold-or-flood decision needs, and no more. The precise schedule — shortest, longest, and preferred intervals — stays in the platform declaration where Layer 0 put it (Layer 0, 7.3); the beacon's single coarse value is what sizes a neighbour's retention patience. The bytes are weighed as 7.1 Note 2 weighs everything: the field appears only in the beacons of hives that sleep, and costs every other beacon nothing.

Note 4 to entry: This declaration closes a chain that was open across three layers. Layer 0 requires a platform to declare its availability, as published text that is never transmitted; nothing carried the same fact hive to hive, so a hive relaying for a group it does not belong to could never learn it, and fell back to flooding for exactly the destinations most likely to be asleep. With the beacon carrying it, the value arrives below the trust boundary, from the air, with no relationship to the emitter — which is what a shared mesh requires. (Settled by ruling, 2026-07-31.)

Verification: present a scanner with a beacon declaring an intermittent duty class and an interval, and the same beacon truncated before the field. It reports intermittent with the interval for the first and unknown for the second — never continuous for the truncated one.

5.5 The class hash

5.5.1 A class hash shall be computed from the class exactly as written.

Note 0 to entry: The algorithm is still unspecified (register finding SS1), and that absence is not neutral: an implementer reaching for the cheap obvious choice reaches for a short non-cryptographic hash, which is what the deployed implementation did — and the attack described at 5.5.2 Note 0 follows from that choice alone. An unspecified algorithm selects its own default, and the default is the weak one. Until the algorithm is pinned, 5.5.3 and 5.5.4 are what stand between a collidable hash and a false label.

5.5.2 A scanner matching on class shall compute the hash of the class it seeks and compare, and shall not attempt to recover the class from the hash.

5.5.3 A scanner that learns a class string — by asking, as 7.2.2 permits — shall not serve that string as the class of any other hive advertising the same hash. A class learned from one hive is that hive's, and nothing else's.

5.5.4 Where a class string learned from the network is displayed to a person or used as a label, a scanner shall not replace a class string it holds on the strength of a hash match alone.

Note 0 to entry: 5.5.3 and 5.5.4 exist because the hash is not collision-resistant and was never meant to carry a name. A deployed implementation cached learned strings keyed by hash and served them for every hive advertising that hash — which is 5.5.2's forbidden step reached by a different route — and because the hash it used was a 32-bit non-cryptographic one, an attacker could craft a string colliding with a well-known class, pass the hash check honestly, and relabel every device of that class on an operator's screen. No key and no delivery were involved: the whole attack is a lie told to a human. (Added 2026-08-02 from a measured finding in the android lane; PROVISIONAL(supervisor).)

Note 3 to entry: The reasoning of Layer 4, 7.2.1 does not transfer here, and the two notes must not be read as one policy. There, a hash match is treated as a match and collisions are survived rather than prevented, because wrong delivery is harmless delivery — a handler that does not understand an event ignores it. A wrong label is not harmless, because a person believes it. Same hash width, same collision, opposite consequence: one is survivable, the other is deception. Where a hash decides delivery, survive the collision; where it decides what a person is told, do not let it decide alone.

Note 1 to entry: The hash supports exact matching and nothing else. A class is structured — a name in reverse-DNS form, with a supplier and a role in it — and hashing destroys the structure completely: two classes differing only in supplier share nothing but chance bits. A scanner looking for "any screen, whoever made it" cannot do so from a beacon, and Clause 7 says where that is done instead.

Note 2 to entry: 5.5.1 says exactly as written because a class is compared as bytes. A difference in case, in separator, or in a trailing character produces a different hash, which produces no match and no error. Two hives whose class strings differ by one character do not disagree about anything; they never see each other.

6 Cadence

6.1 Bearers with a fixed cadence

6.1.1 A hive shall beacon at a stated interval on a bearer whose medium permits a fixed cadence.

6.1.2 Such a hive should shorten the interval where it is moving or expects to be discovered, and lengthen it where it is stationary or short of energy.

6.1.3 A hive shall declare the range of intervals it can adopt.

6.2 Bearers governed by an airtime budget

6.2.1 On a bearer declaring itself regulated at Layer 1, a hive shall treat beacons as the lowest priority traffic.

6.2.2 Such a hive shall not emit a beacon where the budget is exhausted or where any other traffic is waiting.

6.2.3 Such a bearer shall not be given a fixed beacon interval.

Note 1 to entry: 6.2.3 is a requirement and not an omission. On a regulated bearer the cadence is an outcome, not a setting — it falls out of what the budget has left after everything else, so it lengthens by itself as a deployment gets denser or busier. A fixed interval would be a promise the bearer breaks whenever the network is under load, which is precisely when a scanner is most likely to be relying on it.

Note 2 to entry: The consequence for a scanner is that silence on a regulated bearer carries much less information than silence on an unregulated one, and 6.3 is where that is accounted for.

6.3 Cadence and fade

6.3.1 The period after which a peer unheard on a bearer is treated as receding shall exceed the longest interval at which that bearer's beacons can be expected.

6.3.2 On a regulated bearer (6.2), that period shall exceed the longest interval the airtime budget can produce under load, and shall not be derived from the interval observed while the bearer is idle.

6.3.3 A hive shall not apply one bearer's fade behaviour to another bearer.

Note 1 to entry: This clause is what the fade rate declared at Layer 1 is set against. Layer 1 requires each bearer to declare how quickly an unheard peer recedes and does not say what the value should be, because the value is not a property of the medium — it is a property of how often the medium is used to announce, which is decided here.

Note 2 to entry: 6.3.3 is stated because a single fade period across bearers is the obvious simplification and is wrong in both directions at once. Applied to a slow bearer, a fast bearer's impatience declares working peers gone every few minutes; applied to a fast bearer, a slow bearer's patience keeps peers in the table long after they have left. The first costs re-discovery, the second costs frames sent into empty air.

Note 3 to entry: 6.3.2 names the case that makes 6.3.1 insufficient on its own. A regulated bearer observed on a quiet bench announces often; the same bearer in a dense deployment announces rarely. A fade period measured on the bench is a fade period that fails in the field, and it fails by declaring live peers gone at exactly the moment the network is busiest.

Verification: load a regulated bearer to its budget with higher-priority traffic and measure the longest gap between beacons. The declared fade period exceeds it.

7 Discovery

7.1 Progressive filtering

7.1.1 A scanner shall evaluate candidates in the following order, and shall not perform a later step for a candidate that failed an earlier one.

# Question Answered from Cost
1 Is this Reality2? the bearer's discovery marker free, before parsing
2 Is this the class I want? the class hash in the beacon cheap, no connection
3 Is this a class of the kind I want? the class, obtained by asking a query
4 What can it actually do? the capability list, obtained by asking a connection
5 Does it do it for me? performing the thing and observing a full exchange

7.1.2 Steps 1 and 2 shall be answerable from a beacon alone.

7.1.3 Steps 3 and 4 shall not be required in order to complete steps 1 and 2.

Note 1 to entry: The ordering is by cost, and the costs differ by orders of magnitude. Step 1 is a byte comparison against traffic the radio was receiving anyway; step 5 is a round trip that changes state on another hive. A scanner that connects in order to decide whether to connect has spent the expensive step to answer the cheap question, and on a crowded bearer it will spend it on every hive in range.

Note 2 to entry: 7.1.2 is a constraint on the beacon rather than on the scanner. It is what keeps beacons small: a beacon has to fit the tightest advertisement budget of any bearer that carries one, and the same bytes are broadcast forever on media where every byte is airtime. Anything that would grow a beacon is weighed against that, and belongs at step 3 or later if it cannot be afforded.

Note 3 to entry: Step 5 is the only step that establishes anything. Class and capability are claims; performing the act is the test. A scanner needing certainty performs it.

7.2 Where structure is matched

7.2.1 Matching a class by any part of it — a family of classes, a supplier, a role shared across suppliers — shall be performed on the class itself and not on a class hash.

7.2.2 A scanner shall obtain the class by asking, where the bearer provides a means to ask.

Note 1 to entry: This is step 3, and it exists because 5.5 destroyed the structure. On a bearer with no means to ask, discovery stops at exact match, and a scanner on such a bearer cannot find a class it does not already know the exact name of. (Clause 12.)

7.3 Hives that already know each other

7.3.1 A scanner holding an association between a beacon identifier and a peer it already has information about may skip steps 2 to 4 for that peer.

7.3.2 For such a peer, a beacon shall be treated as evidence of presence, and not as new information about class or capability.

Note 1 to entry: For hives that already know each other, the beacon's job changes entirely: it stops being discovery and becomes the answer to is this peer still here. That is the common case in a settled deployment, and it is why beacons keep being emitted long after everyone has found everyone.

8 Capability summary

8.1 A capability summary, where carried, shall admit false positives and shall not admit false negatives.

8.2 A capability summary shall carry the parameters needed to interpret it.

8.3 A scanner shall treat a positive result as a reason to ask, and shall not treat it as an answer.

8.4 A hive publishing no public capability shall not carry a capability summary.

Note 1 to entry: The direction of error in 8.1 is the whole design. A false positive costs a query that finds nothing; a false negative loses a hive that was exactly what was wanted, and loses it silently. The summary is therefore built so that absent is certain and present is a hint.

Note 2 to entry: 8.2 exists because a summary cannot be read without knowing how it was built. Parameters agreed out of band are parameters two implementations will disagree about, and the disagreement appears as a hive that is never found rather than as an error.

Note 3 to entry: 8.4 is not an optimisation. A hive with nothing public to announce carries a summary that answers no to everything, at the cost of the bytes — and on a bearer where carrying it forces a larger advertisement format, the cost is paid by every beacon, for a result that was knowable without it.

9 What Layer 2 provides upward

9.1 Layer 2 shall provide, for each peer it has heard:

a) the peer's identity, where resolvable;

b) each bearer the peer has been heard on;

c) the time of the most recent sighting on each bearer, on the platform's monotonic time source;

d) the peer's class;

e) the peer's capability summary, where one was carried; and

f) the peer's duty class and stated interval, where declared (5.4b).

9.2 Layer 2 shall record a sighting per bearer, and shall not merge sightings on different bearers into one.

9.3 Layer 2 shall not select a bearer for any purpose.

Note 1 to entry: 9.2 is what makes 9.1 c) worth having. A peer heard on three bearers is one peer with three independent freshness values, and merging them loses exactly the information the layers above need — a peer whose long-range bearer is healthy and whose proximity bearer has gone quiet has moved away, and a merged sighting shows only that it was heard recently.

Note 2 to entry: 9.3 draws the line this document is on one side of. Layer 2 establishes which bearers can reach a peer; Layer 3 decides which one to use. The separation matters because the same evidence serves decisions this standard has not specified — and a layer that had already chosen would have discarded the alternatives.

Note 3 to entry: 9.1 c) is measured on the monotonic time source Layer 0 requires, which is comparable only with itself. Two hives cannot compare sightings, and nothing here asks them to.

10 Conformance

Every requirement of this document is demonstrated by conformance evidence of one of the three classes of 01-terminology.md, 3.10 — behavioural, by construction, or by declaration. A requirement whose evidence is by construction is not thereby optional: the demonstration is an inspection a third party can perform without the implementer's cooperation, and its absence is non-conformance exactly as a failed behavioural test is. Behavioural evidence for any requirement of this document carries the control 01-terminology.md 4.2 to 4.5 requires — the same procedure run against a case the requirement excludes, giving a distinguishable non-satisfying result attributable to the requirement under test; a demonstration that could not have come out any other way is not evidence. (Added 2026-08-02, PROVISIONAL(supervisor); the control sentence added the same day, finding SS183.)

10.1 An implementation conforms to this document where it:

a) beacons on every bearer in service and never suspends it (Clause 4);

b) carries what Clause 5 requires and omits what Clause 5 forbids;

c) meets Clause 6 for each bearer's cadence, and declares a fade period satisfying 6.3;

d) filters in the order of 7.1 and answers steps 1 and 2 from a beacon alone;

e) carries a capability summary meeting Clause 8, or carries none; and

f) provides what Clause 9 requires, per bearer.

10.2 Conformance to 5.3 shall be demonstrated by derivation: the candidate transformations of the hive's other identifiers are computed and compared against the beacon identifier, whole and in part.

Note 1 to entry: 10.2 names the method because observation cannot reach what 5.3 forbids: an embedded identifier looks exactly like a random one from outside. Only recomputing the candidate derivations distinguishes an opaque identifier from one that silently carries the identity it was supposed to keep off the air.

11 Security and privacy considerations

11.1 A beacon is public. Anything in it is available to anyone in range, indefinitely, without any relationship to the emitter.

11.2 A beacon is forgeable. An observer can emit beacons resembling any it has heard, and no receiver can distinguish them at this layer.

11.3 A beacon identifier is stable, and a passive observer within range can therefore recognise the same hive across time and, with receivers in more than one place, across places. This standard accepts that, deliberately: what the identifier never yields is who the hive is, what it belongs to, or any identifier usable elsewhere — and the observer's cost is physical presence, which is the bound this standard's privacy position rests on. The protection is opacity and non-aggregation, not unlinkability.

Note 1 to entry: What 11.2 costs an attacker is nothing, and what it gains them is a candidate entry in someone's table. Everything that could be gained by being believed is gated above the trust boundary, which is why forgery is tolerated here rather than defended against.

Note 2 to entry: Two residual exposures are known and are not closed by this document. A class is public, so an observer learns what kinds of device are present in a place even where they can identify none of them. And a hive's pattern of transmission is visible whatever the beacons contain — when it is present, how often it speaks, when it stops. Rotation addresses identity, not activity.

11.4 This standard offers no confidentiality of traffic metadata against any party within range of a bearer. An implementation shall not assume, nor represent to a person, that a peer's declared build mode, its group membership, or its absence from any relationship limits what it can observe.

Note 0 to entry: 11.4 generalises what 11.1 to 11.3 already state about beacons, and what Layer 5, 7.3.4 Note 2 states about a carrier, to the party neither of them names: a listener in range that never relays, never declares and never joins. The grounding is the medium, not anyone's honesty. On a wired network the set of parties is the set on the cable; on a shared radio medium anyone in range is a party, whether anyone knows them or not — so metadata leaves before any question of membership, trust or build mode arises. Relaying does not require decrypting, and observing does not require relaying. (Roy, d231: a device can declare production and report traffic anyway, so the separation was unpoliceable. The medium is the stronger reason: a silent listener does not need to lie — it needs only to be in range and say nothing at all, and nothing declares it, nothing admits it, and nothing can refuse it.) This is why the protection this standard does offer is stated as opacity and non-aggregation (11.3) rather than as confidentiality. (Added 2026-08-04, PROVISIONAL(standard), applying d231.)

12 What is not decided at this layer

This clause states gaps and contains no requirements.

12.1 Matching a class by structure on a bearer with no means to ask. 7.2 puts structural matching after a query. On a bearer that provides no query, the step does not exist — a scanner can match a class it knows exactly and nothing else. Whether such a means should be added, and what it would cost in airtime, is undecided.

12.2 How a scanner behaves when it hears many candidates at once. Nothing here bounds how many peers a scanner tracks, states what it does when it can track no more, or says which sighting it discards. Two implementations in a dense deployment will therefore diverge in which peers they know about, without either being non-conforming.
