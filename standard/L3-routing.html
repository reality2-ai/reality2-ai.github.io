Reality2 Standard — Layer 3: Routing

Status: Draft — this names the editorial maturity of the text, and nothing else. It is not a licence to deviate: every requirement here is normative now and the implementing lanes build from it (d041, Roy: standard is the canon). The only text open to replacement is a value marked PROVISIONAL(...) at its own clause. (Subject stated because a status field is scoped to a question the reader may not be asking, and a claim with an unstated subject will be given the subject the reader brought — composer, 2026-08-03.) Date: 2026-08-03

Verbal forms. shall = requirement · should = recommendation · may = permission · can = possibility and capability.

Introduction

Below this layer a hive has several bearers, each with its own peers, its own reach and its own idea of how quickly silence means something. Layer 3 is where they stop being several things. One table, one view of who is out there, one decision about what to do with each frame.

The thing being routed over does not hold still. Peers appear because someone walked into range and disappear because they went to sleep; a path that worked a minute ago may not exist now, and the hive that would have told you is the one that left. Routing here is therefore not the business of finding a path and following it. It is deciding what to do with a frame given knowledge that is incomplete, out of date by an unknown amount, and partly supplied by strangers.

Four consequences run through every clause below.

Relaying is the default, and it is not conditional on knowing anybody. A hive forwards frames for hives it has never met, belonging to groups it cannot join, carrying contents it cannot read. That is not generosity — it is the only reason a mesh of intermittent devices reaches anything, and a hive that relays only for its own group has built a private network that happens to share a radio.

What a hive knows about a peer has two parts with different trustworthiness, and they are never mixed. That something is out there and can be heard is learned from anything at all, including a forgery. That a particular hive is alive and should be delivered to is learned only from a message that proves it. Clause 4 keeps those apart, and most of the attacks this layer has to survive are attempts to get the first treated as the second.

A frame that cannot be sent now is not necessarily undeliverable. The destination may be asleep, or the path may return. Holding a frame and forwarding it later is an ordinary routing outcome here rather than an exception — which means a hive has to distinguish not reachable yet from not reachable, using evidence that is designed to be forgeable.

Nothing here reads a frame's contents. Every decision in this document is made from the frame's routing fields and from what the hive already knows. A frame carrying something this hive could not decrypt routes exactly like one it could.

1 Scope

This document specifies how a hive maintains its view of reachable peers, how it decides whether and where to forward a frame, which bearer it uses, and what it does with a frame it cannot forward now.

It specifies the suppression of duplicates, the limiting of replication, and the bounds that keep forwarding from being used to amplify traffic.

It specifies what this layer provides to the layers above and requires of the layers below.

It establishes what conformance at this layer consists of, and identifies what is not decided.

It does not specify frame layout, which is Layer 4. It does not specify membership, authorisation, or the decision to deliver a frame to an application, all of which are above the trust boundary. It does not specify any bearer.

3 Terms and definitions

For the purposes of this document, the terms and definitions given in the documents listed in Clause 2 apply, together with the following.

3.1 neighbour

hive from which frames have been received directly, without an intervening hive

3.2 neighbour table

record a hive keeps of its neighbours

3.3 link

evidence that a neighbour can be heard on a bearer, together with how strongly and how recently

3.4 liveness

evidence that a neighbour is present and is entitled to be delivered to, established only by a message whose authenticity has been verified

3.5 confidence

value between 0 and 1 expressing how likely a frame sent a given way is to arrive

3.6 origin

hive that created a frame, as recorded in the frame

3.7 replication budget

number of copies of a frame a hive may put into the network

3.8 custody

holding of a frame by a hive that is neither its origin nor its destination, for forwarding later

Note 1 to entry: Layer 5's wallet companion uses custody for holding key material (L5C-management-wallet.md, 3.2). In this document custody is always of a frame; where the two could be confused, say frame custody and key custody.

3.9 duty class

(defined in Layer 2, 3.9)

Note 1 to entry: The duty class states, hive to hive, the same fact a platform declares as its availability at Layer 0. It travels in the beacon (Layer 2, 5.4b): declared by hives receptive only at intervals, absent from everyone else's beacons, unauthenticated because its forgery is conservative (7.3 Note 3), and unknown where unheard. (This was the open gap 11.2 until 2026-07-31, when a ruling closed it.)

4 The neighbour table

4.1 One peer, one entry

4.1.1 A hive shall keep one neighbour table entry per peer, keyed on the peer's canonical hive identifier.

4.1.2 An entry shall record which bearers the peer has been heard on, and shall hold per-bearer values for link strength and for the time of the most recent reception.

4.1.3 A hive shall form and refresh entries from any received frame and from any received beacon, treating the two as equivalent for this purpose.

Note 1 to entry: 4.1.1 depends on Layer 1 reporting peers by their canonical hive identifier. Where that fails, this table is where the damage appears — one peer becomes several entries, each holding a fraction of the evidence, and every judgement made from the table is made on that fraction.

Note 2 to entry: 4.1.3 draws together two things the layers below report separately: what a bearer can address, and what Layer 2 has heard announce itself. Both are evidence of the same fact and neither is sufficient alone — a peer that beacons but has never sent a frame is real, and so is a peer sending frames that has not beaconed recently.

4.2 Two axes, and the trust boundary runs between them

4.2.1 An entry shall carry a link axis (3.3) and a liveness axis (3.4), and a hive shall not derive one from the other.

4.2.2 The link axis shall be updated by any received frame, whether or not its authenticity has been established.

4.2.2a The entry a received frame updates shall be that of the peer the bearer observed transmitting it, and never an identity the frame itself carries.

4.2.3 The liveness axis shall be updated only by a frame whose authenticity has been verified and whose freshness has been established.

4.2.4 A hive shall not treat the link axis as evidence that a peer is entitled to be delivered to.

Note 1 to entry: This is the trust boundary passing through a data structure, and it is the most consequential rule in this document. Everything below the boundary is TG-agnostic and forms from whatever it hears; everything that decides who gets delivered to sits above it and forms only from what it can verify.

Note 2 to entry: The reason for the split is that the cheap evidence is forgeable and the expensive evidence is not. Anyone can transmit a frame claiming to come from anywhere, and a hive that treats that as proof a peer is alive has let a stranger with a radio decide what it does with other people's traffic. Clause 7 depends on this distinction absolutely.

Note 2a to entry: 4.2.2a states what 4.2.2 leaves open: which entry a frame updates. A frame says who originated it; a neighbour table records who is directly reachable, and the two coincide only at one hop. Keying the update on the frame's origin manufactures a direct-neighbour entry for a hive that may be many hops away and was never heard by this bearer — a link axis populated with things the radio never observed, which is the one thing that axis is for. This is the general rule of 01-terminology.md 5.4 in its clearest form: the origin field is valid, and it does not describe this link. (Added 2026-08-02, PROVISIONAL(supervisor), from an implementation that made the observation an obligation of its own neighbour-table entry point.)

Note 3 to entry: The link axis is not a yes-or-no. It carries how strongly a peer is heard and how recently, and both change continuously. A link that has faded is a routing fact and never a statement about whether a hive is still a member of anything.

4.3 Forming a link is never gated on verification

4.3.1 A hive shall form a link for a peer it has heard, without first verifying anything.

4.3.1a A hive shall not treat having heard a peer as evidence that the peer can hear it.

Note 0 to entry: Hearing establishes the direction the frame arrived in, and nothing else. Radio links are routinely asymmetric — different transmit powers, antennas, noise floors and duty cycles at each end — so a peer heard strongly may be unable to receive at all. 4.3.1 forms a link from hearing because that is the cheap evidence this layer is built on, and 4.2.4 already refuses to read the link axis as entitlement; 4.3.1a closes the remaining misreading, which is direction rather than trust.

Note 0a to entry: 6.2.2's freshness is reverse-direction evidence used as a forward-direction estimate, and that is permitted. A next hop scored by how recently it was heard is a bet, and a good one — the alternative is probing every peer before every forwarding decision, which no bearer budget here can afford. What the score is not is confirmation that the peer received anything: that confirmation exists only above this layer, in a response, and this layer has no acknowledgements by design (Layer 4, 5.2). Layer 1, 5.1 Note 1 states the neighbouring distinction — addressable and heard from are different records and neither substitutes for the other. (Added 2026-08-02, PROVISIONAL(supervisor), finding SS162, from a hardware bring-up where a listen-only probe was read as evidence about a link it could not test.)

4.3.2 A hive implementing Layers 1 to 4 without Layer 5 shall form links and shall relay, and shall not be required to hold any key material in order to do so.

Note 1 to entry: 4.3.1 is stated as a requirement because the opposite is a natural mistake with a specific failure. A hive that forms neighbours only from verified messages has no neighbours at all when it holds no keys — which is exactly the case of a relay carrying traffic for a group it does not belong to. It reports itself as having found nobody, on a medium full of traffic it could have carried.

Note 2 to entry: This is the same property Layer 0 describes from the platform side: a device that holds no key material still participates. Here is where that participation actually happens.

4.4 The cost of forming freely, and what pays it

4.4.1 A hive shall bound the size of its neighbour table.

4.4.2 An entry never supported by verified evidence shall hold a confidence no greater than a stated provisional ceiling, and shall not be raised above it by volume of traffic.

4.4.3 Where the table is full, a hive shall evict the lowest-confidence entry eligible for eviction.

4.4.4 A hive shall not evict an entry supported by verified evidence in order to admit one that is not.

4.4.5 The provisional ceiling shall be strictly below the forwarding threshold (7.2.1): a ceiling equal to the threshold is non-conforming.

4.4.6 Where the table is full and no entry is eligible for eviction, the newcomer shall be refused: a full table of verified entries does not admit an unverified sighting, and a verified entry shall never be evicted to admit one.

Note 5 to entry: 4.4.6 states what 4.4.3 and 4.4.4 already jointly imply, because the implication is met only by an implementer holding both clauses at once — a linear read never meets the case, and the three resolutions an implementer could reach unaided (refuse the newcomer, evict a verified entry anyway, grow the table) differ exactly where it matters: evicting verified presence to admit an unverified claim is the accumulation this whole clause exists to prevent, and an unbounded table is 4.4.1 abandoned. Refusal is the only resolution that keeps both. The refused sighting is not lost forever — the peer keeps announcing, and admission waits for room made honestly, by fade or by departure.

Note 3 to entry: Neither value is given here — both are the deployment's — but the ordering between them is the conformance line, because it is what makes 7.2.3 structural rather than aspirational: with the ceiling strictly below the threshold, a destination supported by unverified evidence alone cannot cross into forwarding, on any conformant implementation, whatever values were chosen. Without the ordering, two implementations choosing differently would disagree about when a destination is reachable and interoperate while doing so — the silent kind of divergence, which no handshake ever surfaces.

Note 4 to entry: Both comparisons are stated strict because the loose readings only bite together, at equality: at-or-below here plus at-or-above at 7.2.1 forwards an unverified entry sitting exactly at its ceiling — the precise accumulation this clause exists to make impossible — while either comparison being strict keeps equality safe. An earlier draft carried "strictly" only in this note, which is a note doing the clause's work; both clauses now say it themselves.

Note 1 to entry: 4.3.1 opens an obvious attack: transmit under many identities and fill the table. The mitigation is not to gate formation on verification — that was the leak. It is that unverified entries occupy a band below verified ones and are evicted first, so the attack costs a real neighbour nothing.

Note 2 to entry: 4.4.2's second half is the part that is easy to omit. An attacker can generate traffic cheaply, so if being heard often raised confidence, the ceiling would buy nothing.

Verification: fill the table with unverified entries while one verified entry is present. The verified entry survives, and no unverified entry exceeds the ceiling however much traffic it generates.

4.5 Fading is per bearer

4.5.1 A hive shall age each link on the bearer it was observed on, using that bearer's declared fade behaviour.

4.5.2 A hive shall not apply one fade period across bearers.

4.5.3 Fading and eviction shall affect routing only, and shall not alter any hive's membership of anything.

Note 1 to entry: 4.5.2 has a specific consequence worth stating: a single fade period evicts every long-range neighbour for being long-range. A sensor on a slow bearer that speaks every few minutes is behaving normally, and a period sized for a fast bearer declares it gone between every pair of transmissions.

Note 2 to entry: 4.5.1 uses the monotonic time source Layer 0 requires, which measures intervals and nothing else. No comparison here is made against another hive's clock, and none is needed.

Note 3 to entry: The fade behaviour applied here is declared by the bearer at Layer 1 and constrained at Layer 2, which requires it to exceed the longest interval at which that bearer's announcements can be expected. Three layers touch one value, and each does a different thing with it: Layer 1 declares it, Layer 2 says what it has to exceed, and this clause applies it.

5 Relaying

5.1 Relaying is the default

5.1.1 A hive shall relay a received frame that is not addressed to it, unless a condition in 5.8 applies.

5.1.2 A hive shall relay without regard to whether it can read the frame, whether it knows the origin, and whether the origin belongs to any group the hive belongs to.

Note 1 to entry: 5.1.2 is what makes one shared mesh possible rather than many private ones. A relay carries ciphertext it cannot read, for hives it cannot identify, into a group it cannot join. Everything protecting the contents is end-to-end and does not involve the relay at all.

5.2 Hop limit

5.2.1 A hive shall decrement a frame's hop limit before relaying it.

5.2.2 A hive shall not relay a frame whose hop limit is zero after decrement.

5.3 Suppressing duplicates

5.3.1 A hive shall discard a frame it has already seen, identified by the pair of the frame's origin and its message identifier.

5.3.2 A hive shall not use the identity of the hive it received the frame from as any part of that key.

5.3.3 A hive shall drop a frame of a type required to carry an origin that does not carry one, and shall not supply the missing origin itself.

5.3.4 A hive shall bound the lifetime of a duplicate-suppression record. A record shall expire without requiring further frames to arrive, and a record whose age cannot be determined shall be treated as expired.

Note -1 to entry: A capacity bound is not a lifetime bound, and only the second satisfies this clause. A fixed-size cache of N records evicts when N further frames arrive — which bounds memory, not time. On a quiet link an entry then persists for hours, and on a link an attacker keeps quiet it persists for as long as they choose. Duplicate suppression is a decision about time, so the property that matters is the one an implementation cannot get from capacity: the suppression ends with no traffic required to flush it. (Stated because an implementation holding a fixed ring reported the silencing attack of 10.5 without noticing its own cache was that attack's best case — its author was looking at the key, "whose origin is this", and not at the duration, "how long does this decision last".)

Note -0a to entry: The unreadable case resolves toward expiry because suppression is the authority here. A record that cannot be aged — Layer 0, 5.2 permits a monotonic clock not to span a power cycle, so an insertion instant can appear to lie in the future — must not go on suppressing: re-flooding a duplicate costs airtime, while suppressing wrongly silences a peer. This is 01-terminology.md 5.1 with the tie-break of its Note 8: the reading that grants something is the permissive one, and here the thing granted is drop this member's frames.

Note 0 to entry: Duplicate suppression is not a security mechanism inside a trust group, and 5.3.4 is what bounds the damage. The key (origin, message identifier) sits wholly inside Layer 4's authenticated span, so no outsider can forge either half — but that span is tagged under the group key, which every member holds. Layer 5, 7.2.2 says the consequence plainly: passage establishes which group, never which member. So a member can emit frames naming another member as origin, with valid tags, and pre-claim that member's (origin, message identifier) pairs — after which every relay suppresses that member's genuine frames as duplicates. Structurally valid, cryptographically verified, and false. A bounded record lifetime makes such suppression temporary rather than permanent, which is the difference between a nuisance and a member able to silence another indefinitely. Clause 10, 10.5 records what remains. (Added 2026-08-02, PROVISIONAL(supervisor), from a sweep for artefacts trusted because they validate.)

Note 1 to entry: 5.3.2 names the mistake that looks correct. The immediate sender is available, obvious, and different at every hive the frame passes through — so keying on it makes duplicate suppression work at one hop and fail at every hop after, and the failure is an expanding flood rather than an error.

Note 2 to entry: 5.3.3's second half closes the same hole from the other side. If two hives receiving the same origin-less frame each supplied their own identity as the origin, the frame would acquire two different origins and every hive downstream would see two distinct frames. Supplying it also makes replies go to the relay rather than to the sender.

Verification: send one frame along two paths that rejoin. Exactly one copy continues past the rejoining hive.

5.4 Three separate duplicate concerns

5.4.1 A hive shall not extend the suppression of 5.3 to frames it is the destination of.

Note 1 to entry: There are three distinct places a duplicate matters, and only the first is this layer's. In transit, a duplicate is wasted transmission and 5.3 removes it. At the destination, a duplicate is a message delivered twice, which is bounded separately and imperfectly. In the effect, a duplicate is something happening twice, and that is made harmless by the receiving application rather than prevented by the network.

Note 2 to entry: The design accepts that duplicates reach destinations. Replication is deliberate — several copies are put into the network precisely so that one arrives — so a mechanism that guaranteed single delivery would be undoing the mechanism that achieves delivery at all. The last line of defence is that acting on a message twice has the same result as acting on it once, and that is stated where it belongs, above this layer.

5.5 Replication budget

5.5.1 A frame shall carry a replication budget.

5.5.2 Where the budget permits forwarding, a hive shall forward with half the budget, rounded down, and retain the remainder.

5.5.3 Where half the budget rounded down is zero, a hive shall not relay the frame, and shall retain it for delivery if the destination becomes directly reachable.

5.5.4 A hive shall not round a zero forwarding budget up.

5.5.5 Where the budget denotes flooding (Layer 4, 4.1.2a: value 15, forwarded unchanged), a hive shall forward to every viable neighbour.

Note 1 to entry: 5.5.3 and 5.5.4 are the same rule stated twice because the second is what gets implemented wrongly. Handing on the last copy feels like forwarding and is the opposite of it — the whole purpose of retaining the final copy is that it stays with a hive that can wait for the destination, instead of being passed to a hive that cannot.

Note 2 to entry: Halving is what makes replication self-limiting. A budget that were passed on undiminished would multiply at every hop, and nothing downstream could tell the difference between a legitimate frame and an amplifier.

5.6 What flooding means

5.6.1 Flooding shall mean that every viable neighbour receives a copy.

5.6.2 A hive shall not treat one transmission as satisfying 5.6.1 without establishing that the bearer's transmission reaches every viable neighbour.

5.6.3 A neighbour shall be viable on a bearer only where that bearer can address it.

Note 1 to entry: 5.6.3 closes a gap between two records that are both correct. A hive can know of a peer from its announcements without any bearer being able to address it — heard, and not reachable. Counting such a peer as viable makes a flood appear to have fallen short of 5.6.1 when the bearer did everything it could, and no rewording of the flood rule fixes it, because the shortfall is in the neighbour set rather than in the transmission.

Note 1 to entry: On a bearer where one transmission is heard by everyone in range, 5.6.1 is satisfied by transmitting once. On a bearer where it is not, transmitting once reaches one peer and reports success — and the difference is invisible at this layer, because both look like a completed send.

Note 2 to entry: Layer 1 requires every bearer to support broadcast, and permits a bearer whose medium lacks it to satisfy that by sending to each peer it knows of. 5.6.2 is the obligation that makes a hive rely on that rather than assume the medium.

Verification: flood on a bearer with no native broadcast, with three neighbours present. All three receive a copy.

5.7 Bounds on replicated frames

5.7.1 A hive shall not replicate a frame whose payload exceeds a stated size limit, and shall drop it instead.

5.7.2 A hive shall limit the rate at which it replicates frames from any one origin, and shall drop frames from an origin exceeding that limit.

5.7.3 5.7.1 and 5.7.2 shall apply to replication only, and shall not apply to a frame being retained under 5.5.3 or delivered locally.

Note 1 to entry: Replication turns one frame into many, so anything that can be replicated can be used to amplify. Without 5.7.1 a single large frame is multiplied by the whole mesh; without 5.7.2 a small one is, repeatedly.

Note 2 to entry: 5.7.2 is only as sound as the origin it keys on. Where the frame's authenticity has not been established, the origin is a claim, and a party willing to vary it evades the limit — so 5.7.2 bounds what one honest-looking origin can do and does not bound a determined attacker. The size limit and the hop limit do not depend on identity and continue to apply.

5.8 When not to relay

5.8.1 A hive shall not relay a frame where:

a) the hop limit is zero after decrement (5.2.2);

b) the frame has already been seen (5.3.1);

c) the frame is required to carry an origin and does not (5.3.3);

d) the replication budget does not permit it (5.5.3);

e) the frame exceeds a bound in 5.7;

f) no bearer remains after the filters of 6.1; or

g) the hive has ceased relaying under the permission Layer 0 grants a power-constrained device, in which case every frame it originates carries the constrained-origin marking.

5.8.2 A hive shall not relay a frame back to the hive it received it from, except where that hive is the destination.

Note 1 to entry: g) is the routing half of a rule stated at Layer 0. Without it, the two documents disagree: Layer 0 permits the smallest devices to stop relaying, and this clause — which says relay unless a listed condition holds — would have made every such device non-conforming. The marking is what keeps the cessation honest: the mesh learns not to route through the device from the frames it originates.

6 Choosing a bearer

6.1 Filter, then score

6.1.1 A hive shall determine the set of bearers on which the next hop is reachable, and shall consider no others.

6.1.2 A hive shall remove from that set every bearer that:

a) has been disabled locally;

b) cannot carry a frame of this size;

c) reports itself unavailable or failed; or

d) reports itself restricted, where the next hop is not one it already carries.

Note 1 to entry: d) is the only one of the four that depends on which peer is being reached. A restricted bearer is fully usable for the peers it already carries and unusable for any other, so removing it outright would abandon working links, and keeping it would select a bearer that cannot accept the next hop.

6.1.3 A hive shall then score the remaining bearers and select the highest scoring.

6.1.4 Where the set is empty, a hive shall treat the frame as having no route by that hop, and shall not use a bearer removed by 6.1.2.

Note 1 to entry: 6.1.4's second half is what makes 6.1.2 a filter rather than a preference. A disabled bearer that would have scored best is still disabled, and an implementation that reaches for it when nothing else remains has made the local decision advisory.

6.2 What the score accounts for

6.2.1 A score shall increase with the confidence that the bearer reaches the next hop, and shall decrease with the cost of sending on it.

6.2.2 A score shall account for how recently the next hop was heard on that bearer, per bearer.

6.2.3 A hive may bias the score by its own energy state and by any charge associated with a bearer.

Note 1 to entry: 6.2.2 is stated separately because a single freshness value across bearers cannot express the thing being decided. A peer heard a minute ago on a short-range bearer and an hour ago on a long-range one is telling you it has moved, and one number averages that away.

6.3 One bearer per directed frame

6.3.1 Where a hive forwards a frame to one named next hop, it shall transmit on the one bearer it selected.

6.3.2 6.3.1 shall not apply to flooding, which is governed by 5.6.

Note 1 to entry: Transmitting a directed frame on every bearer at once looks like thoroughness and is waste with a cost borne by others: it spends the airtime budget of regulated bearers, and it invites relaying on bearers where the destination was never reachable.

7 Holding frames until a path returns

7.1 When to hold

7.1.1 A hive that finds no viable next hop toward a frame's destination may retain the frame and forward it when a path to that destination returns.

7.1.2 The condition for retaining shall be that the destination is unreachable, and shall not be that the hive has no neighbours.

7.1.3 A hive shall not retain a frame that no bearer in service could carry — one exceeding the maximum transport unit payload of every bearer the hive has, or barred from every one of them by the tier rules of Layer 4. Such a frame shall be dropped at once, with a reason distinct from every transient one.

Note 1 to entry: 7.1.2 names a trigger that never fires. A hive with neighbours but no path to the destination is the ordinary case, and a rule conditioned on having no neighbours at all would leave it dropping exactly the frames this clause exists to save.

Note 2 to entry: 7.1.3 separates two conditions that arrive looking identical. No viable next hop is usually transient — the destination is asleep, the path will return, and retaining is the right act. But a frame that arrived on a large-payload bearer at a hive whose only onward bearer is small is structurally undeliverable by this hive: no returning path changes what its radios can carry, so retention is a slow silent drop that spends buffer a genuinely sleeping destination needed. Waiting only helps when time is what's missing.

Note 3 to entry: Nothing reports this to the origin — delivery is fire-and-forget at every layer, and there is no error return in this standard, deliberately. An origin that needs to know contracts for a receipt with its counterpart, and the absence of the contracted receipt is the signal — detected by the party that cares, at the grade the purpose warranted, exactly as every other loss on this network is detected. The systemic answer to size is upstream: payloads are sized to their tier, and anything larger is divided at its source into ordinary frames (Layer 4, 6.2).

7.2 When to forward what is held

7.2.1 A hive shall forward a retained frame when a path to its destination is established with confidence strictly greater than the threshold used for forwarding; at equality it shall not forward.

7.2.2 A hive shall not treat the existence of an entry for the destination as establishing a path.

7.2.3 A hive shall not treat a destination as reachable, for the purposes of 7.1 or 7.2, on the strength of unverified evidence alone.

Note 1 to entry: 7.2.3 closes an attack that works in both directions, and both outcomes are silent. Forged presence that makes a destination look reachable stops frames being retained for it — they are sent toward a hive that is not there and die. Forged presence that arrives later releases frames that were correctly held — they leave the one hive that could have delivered them, and die somewhere else. Either way nothing is reported, and the destination simply never receives anything.

Note 2 to entry: 7.2.2 is the weaker form of the same mistake made honestly. A faded entry still exists; treating existence as reachability makes a hive release its held frames toward a peer that has been gone for hours.

Verification: transmit unverified frames claiming to originate from an absent destination. Frames for that destination continue to be retained, and nothing held is released.

7.3 Whether to flood or to hold

7.3.1 Where a hive has no confident path to a destination but does have other neighbours, it shall decide between flooding and retaining on what it knows of the destination.

7.3.2 Where the destination is unknown, or is known and continuously receptive, a hive should flood.

7.3.3 Where the destination is known to be receptive only at intervals, and its silence is within what its bearer's fade behaviour expects, a hive should retain rather than flood.

Note 1 to entry: The reason is physical rather than a matter of policy. A flood cannot discover a path to a device whose radio is off. Every copy is transmitted, none is heard, and on a regulated bearer the attempt has spent budget that the destination's next waking would have needed.

Note 2 to entry: The decision is made where the knowledge is — at the hive that neighbours the sleeping destination, which is the only one holding an entry for it. A hive further away has no entry, treats the destination as unknown, and floods, which is correct: it is trying to reach the neighbour that will do the holding.

Note 3 to entry: A duty class is asserted by the hive it describes and is not verified. The exposure is bounded in both directions: claiming to be intermittent delays only that hive's own traffic, and claiming to be continuous costs a flood already limited by hop count and replication budget. Neither gains an attacker anything from anyone else. This is the test that licensed carrying the value in the beacon (Layer 2, 5.4b Note 2), which is how it arrives here.

7.4 Bounds

7.4.1 A hive shall bound the number of frames it retains, and shall discard the oldest first when that bound is reached.

7.4.2 A hive shall discard a retained frame whose hop limit or lifetime has expired.

7.4.3 The period for which a frame is retained should exceed the longest interval for which the destination is expected to be unreceptive.

Note 1 to entry: 7.4.3 is where holding fails quietly if it is got wrong. A retention period shorter than a sleeping destination's sleep discards every frame just before it could have been delivered — the mechanism runs, the buffer fills and empties, and nothing arrives.

7.5 Forwarding what was held

7.5.1 A hive forwarding a retained frame shall transmit it directly, and shall not re-apply the duplicate suppression of 5.3 to it.

7.5.2 A retained frame shall keep the origin and message identifier it arrived with.

Note 1 to entry: 7.5.1 exists because the frame was recorded as seen when it first arrived, and duplicate records are short-lived while retention is not. Re-checking therefore fails in one of two ways depending only on how long the destination slept: soon, and the frame is discarded as a duplicate of itself; later, and the record has expired so the frame is treated as new and floods again. Suppression governs what is admitted, not what is released.

7.6 Holding is not reading

7.6.1 The inability to read a frame shall not be a bar to retaining or forwarding it, and no relationship with the origin or the destination shall be required in order to do either.

Note 1 to entry: This clause is non-discrimination, not a mandate: whether to retain remains the permission of 7.1.1 — this clause only forbids answering that question differently for frames the hive cannot read. (Reworded 2026-08-01, ruling on a conflict with 7.1.1 — the earlier text read as mandating retention.)

8 What this layer provides and requires

8.1 This layer shall provide to the layers above:

a) for each known peer, which bearers it is reachable on and with what confidence;

b) whether a frame has been accepted for forwarding, retained, or dropped, and for which reason; and

c) delivery to the layers above of frames addressed to this hive.

8.2 This layer shall require of the layers below:

a) peers reported by their canonical hive identifier (Layer 1);

b) a per-bearer declaration of reach, cost, wire tier and fade behaviour (Layer 1);

c) per-bearer state and link quality, kept current (Layer 1);

d) per-bearer sightings of peers that have announced themselves (Layer 2); and

e) a monotonic time source (Layer 0).

8.3 This layer shall not require the layers below to interpret any frame.

Note 1 to entry: 8.1 b) is a requirement because the reasons are not equivalent and the difference is invisible from outside. A frame dropped as a duplicate, a frame dropped for exceeding a bound, and a frame retained for a sleeping destination are three different outcomes that all look like "it did not arrive" to anyone downstream.

9 Conformance

Every requirement of this document is demonstrated by conformance evidence of one of the three classes of 01-terminology.md, 3.10 — behavioural, by construction, or by declaration. A requirement whose evidence is by construction is not thereby optional: the demonstration is an inspection a third party can perform without the implementer's cooperation, and its absence is non-conformance exactly as a failed behavioural test is. Behavioural evidence for any requirement of this document carries the control 01-terminology.md 4.2 to 4.5 requires — the same procedure run against a case the requirement excludes, giving a distinguishable non-satisfying result attributable to the requirement under test; a demonstration that could not have come out any other way is not evidence. (Added 2026-08-02, PROVISIONAL(supervisor); the control sentence added the same day, finding SS183.)

9.1 An implementation conforms to this document where it:

a) maintains a neighbour table meeting Clause 4, including the separation of the two axes and the eviction priority;

b) relays by default and suppresses only for the reasons in 5.8;

c) suppresses duplicates on origin and message identifier alone;

d) limits replication as Clause 5 requires, including the bounds in 5.7;

e) selects bearers by filtering then scoring, as Clause 6 requires;

f) retains and releases frames as Clause 7 requires, including the requirement for verified evidence in 7.2.3; and

g) provides and requires what Clause 8 states.

9.2 Conformance to 4.2 and 7.2.3 shall be demonstrated against unverified traffic asserting the conditions each clause turns on.

Note 1 to entry: 9.2 names the only test that reaches these clauses. An implementation that never receives a forgery satisfies them trivially and has not been tested at all, and both are clauses whose failure produces no error — only frames that do not arrive.

10 Security considerations

10.1 This layer forms neighbours from unverified evidence by design. Everything derived from that evidence alone is provisional, and an attacker within radio range can create as many provisional entries as they are willing to transmit for.

10.2 What that attacker gains is bounded by three things and nothing else: provisional entries cannot displace verified ones (4.4.4), cannot be promoted by volume (4.4.2), and cannot make a destination look reachable for the purposes of retention (7.2.3).

10.3 Frame contents are not protected by this layer. A relay handles frames it cannot read, and confidentiality and integrity are established between origin and destination without any relay's participation.

10.4 The rate limit of 5.7.2 depends on an origin that may not have been verified, and is therefore a bound on nuisance rather than on a determined attacker.

10.5 Duplicate suppression binds an outsider and not a member. Its key is unforgeable outside the group and freely forgeable within it, so a member can suppress another member's frames for as long as a duplicate record survives; 5.3.4 bounds that, and nothing at this layer removes it. What a compromised member gains is therefore silence-anyone, not merely send-rubbish, and an operation that must not be suppressible needs the member-attributable evidence of Layer 5, 7.4 and retransmission under a fresh identifier.

Note 1 to entry: The bounds that do not depend on identity — hop limit, replication budget, payload size — are the ones that hold against an attacker who varies what they claim to be. They are deliberately crude, and they are the reason a hive that believes nothing it hears is still safe to run.

11 What is not decided at this layer

This clause states gaps and contains no requirements.

11.1 The ceiling and the threshold — the relationship is settled (2026-08-01); the values never were owed. What this gap recorded as undecided was the relationship, and 4.4.5 now pins it: the provisional ceiling is below the forwarding threshold, so unverified evidence can never cross into forwarding on any conformant implementation. The two values themselves are the deployment's, and divergence in them is quality, not conformance. (Settled by ruling.)

11.6 Build-mode carriage, and the only-connector exception, have no expression at this layer. Layer 2 states the development/production boundary and its one exception — a development hive may carry a production network's traffic where it is the only connector (L2 5.4a, Notes 3 and 4) — but that is an exception to carriage, and this document, which performs carriage, currently says nothing about build mode at all: 5.1.2 relays without regard to origin or group, and no clause conditions next-hop or flood choice on a neighbour's declared mode. An implementer reading this document alone relays development traffic unconditionally and is conformant — which is exactly the state this paragraph exists to mark. The mechanical form — when a differing-mode neighbour may be selected, and how "no same-mode path exists" is established — is register item Q21, awaiting decision.

11.2 How a hive learns a peer's duty class — settled at Layer 2 (2026-07-31). The chain this clause recorded as broken is closed one layer below where it was found: the beacon carries the duty class (Layer 2, 5.4b), declared by the hives that sleep and absent from everyone else's, so a hive relaying for a group it does not belong to learns hold-or-flood from the air, below the trust boundary. 7.3 now turns on a value that arrives; where none has, the duty class is unknown and 7.3.2 applies, which is what it did before. (Settled by ruling, 2026-07-31.)

11.3 What a hive does with its own frames before they are first transmitted. Clause 7 governs frames received from others. A frame a hive originates while it has no viable bearer is not covered by any clause here — whether it is queued, dropped, or subject to the same bounds is unspecified.

11.4 The size of the retention buffer and the retention period. 7.4 requires both to be bounded and 7.4.3 states the relationship the period should satisfy. Neither is given a value, and the interval it should exceed is declared by the destination rather than known to the hive doing the retaining.

11.5 Aggregate rate limiting. 5.7.2 bounds one origin at one hive. Nothing bounds the total volume a determined origin can put through the mesh by spreading it across many frames that are each within every limit.

11.7 The duplicate-record lifetime. 5.3.4 requires the lifetime to be bounded and gives no value. The trade is a deployment's: too long and a member can silence another for that long (10.5); too short and genuine duplicates re-flood the mesh. An implementation should require the value to be chosen rather than supply a default — a silent default is the unexamined value that makes a bound decorative, and it would be chosen once, by whoever wrote the library, for every deployment at once.
