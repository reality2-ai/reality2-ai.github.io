Reality2 Standard — Layer 4: Wire protocol

It is not a licence to deviate: every requirement here is normative now and the implementing lanes build from it (d041, Roy: standard is the canon). The only text open to replacement is a value marked PROVISIONAL(...) at its own clause. (Subject stated because a status field is scoped to a question the reader may not be asking, and a claim with an unstated subject will be given the subject the reader brought — composer, 2026-08-03.) Date: 2026-08-04

Verbal forms. shall = requirement · should = recommendation · may = permission · can = possibility and capability.

Introduction

Everything below this layer moves bytes; everything above it acts on meaning. Layer 4 is the bytes themselves — the one frame format every bearer carries, every relay forwards, and every hive parses, whatever hardware it is and whoever made it.

This is the layer where two implementations meet. A defect anywhere else degrades one hive; a defect here means two conformant-looking implementations exchange bytes and read different messages out of them, silently, forever. So this document behaves differently from the ones below it: it is exact where they are general. Field widths, byte order, bit positions and hash constants are stated as numbers, because prose about bytes is always ambiguous and a test vector is the only unambiguous statement of a wire format there is.

Three design commitments shape the format.

Two tiers, not one. A radio delivering two hundred bytes per transmission and a socket delivering sixty-five thousand cannot carry the same header without one of them paying for the other. So there is a compact tier whose every byte was argued over, and an extended tier that spends bytes to gain width — same fields, same meanings, different sizes. Which tier a frame is parsed at is decided by the bearer it arrived on, not by the frame — the two tiers are deliberately indistinguishable from their bytes alone, and Clause 9 is about what that costs and buys.

The frame carries everything a relay needs and nothing a relay wants. A relay reads the hop limit, the replication budget, the addressing and the route — and cannot read the payload, cannot verify the integrity tag, and cannot tell what the frame is for. That is the trust boundary expressed as a byte layout: the fields a relay may touch are exactly the fields outside the authenticated span.

Integrity is end-to-end or it is nothing. The tag at the end of a frame is computed by the origin and checked by the destination; every hive in between forwards it opaquely. No relay adds trust and no relay can remove it undetected — and what the tag covers is chosen field by field, because the covered fields are the immutable ones and the mutable ones are exactly what relays exist to change.

1 Scope

This document specifies the frame format carried by every bearer: the header, the two tiers, the message types, the addressing model, the identifier hashing, the payload encoding, and the end-to-end integrity envelope.

It specifies what a relay may change in a frame and what it may not.

It specifies how a frame passes, or does not pass, between bearers of different tiers.

It establishes what conformance at this layer consists of, including the place of test vectors, and identifies what is not decided.

It does not specify how frames are delimited on any medium, which is each bearer's binding document. It does not specify routing policy, which is Layer 3, or key material and its derivation, which is Layer 5.

3 Terms and definitions

For the purposes of this document, the terms and definitions given in the documents listed in Clause 2 apply, together with the following.

3.1 frame

unit of data exchanged at this layer: one header, optional route, optional payload, optional integrity tag

3.2 tier

one of the two frame encodings, compact or extended

3.3 authenticated span

fields of a frame covered by its integrity tag

3.4 route record

ordered list carried in a frame of the hives it has passed through, beginning with its origin

3.5 identifier hash

32-bit value derived from a name by the hash of Clause 7, standing in for the name on the wire

4 The frame

4.1 Every frame begins the same way

4.1.1 Byte 0 of every frame shall be, from most significant bit to least:

Bits Field Meaning
7–6 version protocol version; this document specifies version 0
5–3 type message type (Clause 5)
2–0 flags tier-defined (4.2, 4.3)

4.1.2 Byte 1 of every frame shall be: hop limit in bits 7–4, replication budget in bits 3–0.

4.1.2a Budget values 0 to 14 are bounded budgets under Layer 3's halving. Value 15 denotes flooding (Layer 3, 5.5.5): it shall be forwarded unchanged, and the frame's bounds are the hop limit, duplicate suppression, and the limits of Layer 3, 5.7.

Note 1a to entry: A flooding frame is the one deliberate case of an undiminished budget, and it is safe for exactly the reasons Layer 3 states: the hop limit bounds its depth, the duplicate cache bounds its breadth, and the rate limits bound its origin. The cost of the sentinel is that the largest bounded budget is 14 — a copy count no deployment has approached. Before this value was assigned, Layer 3's flood denotation had no wire form at all: every value 0–15 was already meaningful under halving, so the assignment removes one budget from the arithmetic rather than inventing a wider field.

4.1.3 A hive receiving a frame whose version it does not implement shall discard it without parsing further.

Note 1 to entry: The version field is two bits and is also the algorithm epoch: a version names not only a layout but the integrity algorithm and key derivation in force (Clause 10). Two bits is four versions ever, which is deliberate — versions are for incompatible changes, and incompatible changes to a wire format are meant to be nearly impossible to justify.

4.2 The compact tier

4.2.1 A compact frame shall be laid out as:

Offset Size Field
0 1 byte 0 (4.1.1)
1 1 hop limit, replication budget (4.1.2)
2 2 message identifier, 16-bit
4 4 event identifier hash
8 4 target (Clause 6)
12 1 route record count byte (8.4), where the route flag is set
13 4 × count route record entries (8.4), where the route flag is set
… 0–N payload
end 8 integrity tag, where the integrity flag is set

4.2.2 The compact flags shall be: bit 2, route record present; bit 1, integrity tag present; bit 0, constrained-origin marking.

4.2.3 All multi-byte fields shall be big-endian.

4.2.4 A compact frame shall carry no payload length field, and a bearer carrying compact frames shall provide frame boundaries.

Note 1 to entry: 4.2.4 is a stated dependency on Layer 1, not an omission. The length that would delimit the payload is exactly the length the bearer already knows — a radio frame has a size, a delimited stream segment has a size — and carrying it again would spend two bytes of every frame confirming something the medium said. The cost is that compact frames cannot be concatenated without external framing, and that is accepted.

Note 2 to entry: Bit 0 is the marking required by Layer 0's relay rule: a device that has ceased relaying marks every frame it originates. The bit means "the origin will not relay for you", never "the origin is small" — a fully capable hive that happens to run on a microcontroller does not set it.

4.3 The extended tier

4.3.1 An extended frame shall be laid out as:

Offset Size Field
0 1 byte 0 (4.1.1)
1 1 hop limit, replication budget (4.1.2)
2 4 message identifier, 32-bit
6 4 event identifier hash
10 4 payload length, 32-bit
14 8 target: group half and hive half (Clause 6)
22 1 route record count byte (8.4), where the route flag is set
23 8 × count route record entries (8.4), where the route flag is set
… N payload, of exactly payload length bytes
end 32 integrity tag, where the integrity flag is set

4.3.2 The extended flags shall be as 4.2.2.

4.3.3 A hive shall reject as malformed an extended frame whose actual remaining length disagrees with its payload length field. The payload length field counts the payload alone — never the route record, never the integrity tag: after the fixed fields and the route record its count byte declares, the bytes remaining shall be exactly the payload length plus, where the integrity flag is set, the 32 tag bytes.

Note 1 to entry: The two tiers differ in exactly three ways: identifier width, target width, and self-framing. Everything else — byte 0, the hop and budget nibbles, the flag meanings, the hash — is identical, which is what makes a frame's meaning preserved across a tier boundary even though its bytes are not.

4.4 What a relay may change

4.4.1 A relay shall decrement the hop limit, shall adjust the replication budget as Layer 3 requires, and shall append its own identity to the route record.

4.4.2 A relay shall change nothing else. In particular it shall not alter the message identifier, the event identifier hash, the target, the first entry of the route record, the payload, or the integrity tag.

Note 1 to entry: 4.4.1 lists everything, and the list is short by design. The mutable fields are exactly the fields outside the authenticated span (10.2), so a relay never has to choose between doing its job and preserving the tag — the fields it may touch were left out of the tag on purpose.

5 Message types

5.1 The type field shall carry one of:

Value Name Carries
0 EVENT an event to a hive, a group, or broadcast
1 — reserved; never generated, silently discarded on receipt
2 REPLY a response retracing a prior EVENT
3 CAPABILITY the event hashes a hive or group handles
4 GROUP_MGMT group membership traffic; payload opaque at this layer
5 HEARTBEAT liveness and routing exchange between hives that know each other
6–7 — reserved

5.2 Every type shall be fire-and-forget at this layer. Where delivery has to be more probable than one transmission makes it, the sender shall retransmit the same frame, unchanged, and the receiver's duplicate handling shall make retransmission harmless.

5.3 A GROUP_MGMT payload shall not be parsed at this layer, and a GROUP_MGMT frame shall be compact on every bearer.

5.4 A REPLY shall carry the message identifier of the request it answers, with the top bit set; a request shall be originated with the top bit clear.

Note 1 to entry: 5.2 is the delivery model of the whole protocol stated once: there are no acknowledgements at this layer, on any type. Reliability, where needed, is idempotent retransmission — send it again; a receiver that saw it already discards it — which works precisely because 5.4 and Clause 6 of Layer 3 make duplicates identifiable and harmless.

Note 2 to entry: 5.4 partitions the identifier space so that a reply can never collide with a request even at a receiver whose duplicate handling has been mis-built to ignore origin. A correctly built receiver never needs the partition; the partition is there for the incorrectly built one, because the failure it prevents — a reply discarded as a duplicate of its own request — silently removes the return half of every exchange.

Note 3 to entry: CAPABILITY is an optimisation and never a gate. A hive receiving an event it never advertised accepts the frame and simply matches nothing — advertising is a courtesy to senders, not a contract with them.

Note 4 to entry: The type field tells the network how to treat a frame, never what the frame means. An EVENT carrying a sensor reading and an EVENT carrying an opaque chunk of application data are indistinguishable to every layer of this standard — same routing, same relay, same gate. What the network sends is frames; being an event, or being data, is a fact that exists only at the endpoints, where the event identifier selects what handles the payload and the handler decides what the payload is. Nothing in this standard gives the network a reason to know the difference, and nothing above should be built as though it did.

6 Addressing

6.1 The model

6.1.1 A frame shall be addressed to all hives, to one group, or to one hive. No smaller unit shall be addressable on the wire.

6.1.2 The compact target shall be four bytes: zero for broadcast, otherwise the identifier hash of a group or of a hive.

6.1.3 The extended target shall be eight bytes: a group half and a hive half, each four bytes, zero meaning any.

6.1.4 The all-ones value shall be reserved in both tiers and never assigned.

Note 1 to entry: What is deliberately absent here is any addressing of the things a hive contains. The wire stops at the hive — where a message goes once it arrives is the receiving hive's own affair, invisible to the network and different on every implementation. This is why the address fits in four bytes and why nothing above this layer can be addressed around the hive that hosts it.

Note 2 to entry: A compact target is one hash that could name a group or a hive, and the frame does not say which. The receiver resolves it against what it knows — its own hive identity, its group memberships — and ambiguity between a group and a hive hash is a collision, handled as Clause 7 handles all collisions.

6.2 No fragmentation

6.2.1 No layer of this standard shall fragment a frame, and no frame shall reference another frame as its continuation.

6.2.2 A payload too large for one frame shall be divided at its source, above this layer, by the endpoint that knows what the payload means, into payloads each carried by an ordinary, independent frame.

Note 1 to entry: This is the frame principle's carriage rule. The network never holds half a thing: every frame is complete, routable and droppable on its own, and assembly — where a purpose needs it — is an endpoint affair under an endpoint contract, with the chunked-transfer model (Layer 6, 5.3.5) as its worked form. A fragmentation layer would reintroduce everything the core premise forbids: state in the middle, loss that cascades, and a frame whose meaning depends on another frame arriving.

7 Names on the wire

7.1 One hash, stated exactly

7.1.1 Every identifier hash shall be FNV-1a, 32-bit: offset basis 0x811C9DC5, prime 0x01000193, applied byte-wise — exclusive-or, then multiply modulo 2³².

7.1.2 Before hashing, a name shall be UTF-8, stripped of leading and trailing whitespace — internal whitespace preserved — and case-folded by exactly the following mapping, and by no other. Whitespace, for the trim and for nothing else, is exactly this closed set: U+0009–U+000D, U+0020, U+0085, U+00A0, U+1680, U+2000–U+200A, U+2028, U+2029, U+202F, U+205F, U+3000. A name that is empty after the trim is not a name and shall not be hashed. The fold:

a) code points U+0041 to U+005A: add 0x20;

b) code points U+00C0 to U+00DE, except U+00D7: add 0x20;

c) even code points in U+0100 to U+0137 and in U+014A to U+0177: add 1;

d) odd code points in U+0139 to U+0148 and in U+0179 to U+017E: add 1;

e) U+0178: replace with U+00FF;

f) every other code point: unchanged.

Note -1 to entry: The trim set is enumerated for the same reason the fold is arithmetic: "whitespace" without a list is a Unicode-version dependency wearing a common word. The set is the White_Space property's long-stable membership, frozen here by enumeration — an invisible character at a name's edge (a pasted no-break space) trims away as a person would expect, while the same character inside a name is part of it.

Note 0 to entry: The fold is bounded and closed: ASCII, the Latin-1 Supplement, and Latin Extended-A — which covers the macron vowels of te reo Māori (Ā Ē Ī Ō Ū fold under c) exactly as A–Z fold under a)) and the common European diacritics, and nothing else, ever. It is deliberately not "Unicode lowercase": it names no Unicode version, needs no table — every rule is arithmetic — and its behaviour on every code point is decidable from this clause alone, on the smallest device, forever. A name in a script outside the fold is a valid name whose case is significant, which is stated rather than implied. (Ruled 2026-08-01, superseding both an underspecified "lowercased" here and the deployed ASCII-only fold — migration M8.)

7.1.3 Hash 0x00000000 shall mean no event and shall be the event field's value for HEARTBEAT and GROUP_MGMT; hash 0xFFFFFFFF shall never be used.

7.1.4 7.1.2 governs the hashing of names — event names, class strings, and any other human-authored text standing behind an identifier hash. The wire identity of a hive or a group is not a name: it derives from identity material (the persona's member identity and the group's identity, Layer 5, 4.2 and 5.1), by a derivation the formats companion pins, and no case fold or whitespace rule applies to it.

Note 1 to entry: The boundary matters because the two derivations fail differently. A name is typed by people, so its hash forgives the variations people produce; identity material is bytes no person types, so "normalising" it is meaningless — and deriving a hive's identity from a human-typed display name is doubly wrong: it makes the identity guessable by dictionary against the hash, and it couples who a hive is to what someone labelled it. A deployed practice of hashing an operator-supplied display name into a hive identifier is superseded by this clause and Layer 5's identity split — migration M9.

Note 1 to entry: 7.1.2 exists because two parties hashing the same name differently get no error — they get silence. The sender hashes what it wrote, the receiver hashes what it expected, the values differ by a stray capital or a trailing space, and the frame arrives and matches nothing. Normalisation makes the obvious variations of a name hash alike; it cannot make two different names hash alike, and does not try.

7.2 Collisions are survived, not prevented

7.2.1 A hive shall treat a hash match as a match, and shall not attempt to detect collisions on the wire.

7.2.2 Whatever acts on a delivered event shall tolerate parameters that do not fit what it expected.

Note 1 to entry: A 32-bit hash over a deployment's names collides rarely and eventually. When it does, an event is delivered somewhere unintended, and the design's answer is that this is survivable rather than impossible — the receiving handler ignores what it does not understand, and fire-and-forget means nothing waited on the outcome. This is also the answer for the group-or-hive ambiguity of 6.1.2: wrong delivery is harmless delivery.

8 The route record

8.1 The origin of a frame of type EVENT, REPLY, CAPABILITY or HEARTBEAT shall write its own identity as the first entry of the route record before first transmission.

8.2 Each relay shall append its identity to the route record, to a maximum of eight entries; a relay finding the record full shall forward without appending.

8.3 A hive receiving a frame of a type listed in 8.1 with no route record — the route flag clear, or a route record of count zero, which is origin-less — shall drop it, and shall not supply an origin on its behalf. (Count-zero equivalence ruled 2026-08-01, consistent with 10.1.4: a record with no entry zero carries no origin.)

8.4 On the wire, a route record shall be one count byte followed by exactly that many entries. The count shall be 0 to 8; a receiver shall reject as malformed a frame whose count exceeds 8 or whose remaining length cannot contain the counted entries. An entry shall be: at the compact tier, the four-byte identifier hash of a hive; at the extended tier, eight bytes — the hive's group half and hive half as Clause 6 lays them out.

8.5 The count byte is mutable at every hop — appending an entry increments it — and shall be outside the authenticated span; the first entry remains inside it (10.2).

Note 0 to entry: The count byte is what makes a compact frame decodable at all. The compact tier has no payload length field (4.2.4), so a count-less route record and the payload would share one undelimited region — the same bytes readable as one entry plus payload or two entries plus a shorter one, with no error anywhere. One byte retires the ambiguity; 12.2 requires the vector that proves it retired.

Note 1 to entry: The route record serves two masters with one field. Entry zero is the frame's identity — half of the duplicate-suppression key, the destination of any reply, and inside the authenticated span. The tail is a breadcrumb trail — how a reply finds its way back, mutable at every hop and authenticated nowhere. The same list, and the two parts could hardly be more different in what depends on them.

Note 2 to entry: GROUP_MGMT is the one type absent from 8.1, and its absence is why 8.3 cannot be stated as all frames. Its carriage is one hop, its origin is established by the means Layer 5 specifies, and a relay never forwards it as-is.

9 Tiers and bearers

9.1 The bearer decides the tier

9.1.1 A hive shall parse an ordinary frame at the tier its arrival bearer declares (the wire tier of the bearer's Layer 1 profile).

9.1.2 A frame of type GROUP_MGMT shall be parsed as compact on every bearer.

9.1.3 A bearer shall carry ordinary frames of one tier only.

Note 1 to entry: There is nothing in the bytes to decide it with, and that is deliberate: a tier discriminator in the frame would spend a bit of every frame ever sent to solve a problem that assigning tiers to bearers solves for free. The cost is 9.1.3, and the hazard behind it is asymmetric — measured, not assumed (twelve mis-tiered samples, every one rejected; a sample, never a proof): the extended tier is self-framing (4.3.1's payload length), so mis-tiered bytes read there almost never agree with their own length and die at 4.3.3 — a self-framing tier defends itself against accidental mis-tiering. The compact tier trusts its bearer for its boundary (4.2.4) and has no such defence: an extended frame read as compact can yield a well-formed-looking frame — the type field, hop limit and budget sit at the same offsets at both tiers, and a set route flag supplies a plausible route record — whose garbage no parser discovers; a downstream guard sometimes does (8.3's originless drop), and sometimes nothing does, and the frame is relayed. Self-framing also defends only against accident, never against construction: bytes crafted to satisfy the length check parse at either tier. This asymmetry is 4.2.4's stated dependency on Layer 1 surfacing as a security property, and it is the load-bearing reason the tier discipline lives above the parser: where the compact tier is in use, nothing below the bearer assignment can save the frame. The one exception, 9.1.2, is safe because the type field is in the same place at both tiers — readable before the tier is known.

9.2 Crossing between tiers

9.2.1 An unsigned frame forwarded from a bearer of one tier to a bearer of the other shall be re-encoded at the destination tier, preserving every field's value.

9.2.2 A compact message identifier crossing to extended shall be zero-extended; an extended identifier crossing to compact shall not be truncated — a frame whose identifier does not fit the destination tier shall not cross.

9.2.3 A frame bearing an integrity tag shall not cross a tier boundary.

9.2.4 A route record entry crossing from the compact tier to the extended tier shall be widened by zero-extension at the low side: the four compact bytes become the hive half, and the group half shall be zero.

Note 1 to entry: The direction is deliberate and matches 9.2.2's identifier rule: a value widens by gaining zero high bytes, never by shifting. A deployed transcoder that widened high-side (entry << 16) is superseded — migration M10 — and the divergence is intentional, recorded in the cross-check's intentional-divergence series.

9.2.5 An extended target with both halves nonzero shall not cross to the compact tier; one with exactly one half nonzero crosses as that half. A non-broadcast compact target shall not cross to the extended tier — which half it names cannot be known. A route record entry whose group half is zero crosses to compact as its hive half; one whose group half is nonzero shall not cross, and a frame whose route record cannot be re-encoded shall not cross.

Note 1 to entry: The rule is refusal over guessing, parallel to 9.2.2's unfittable identifier — and the asymmetry is honest: narrowing is decidable per value (a zero half says which half the other is), widening of an ambiguous hash is not (a compact hash never said which half it names). Broadcast crosses freely in both directions. (Ruled 2026-08-01, as drafted on the register.)

Note 2 to entry: 9.2.2 and 9.2.5 are one rule in four places. The message identifier (9.2.2), the target, the route record entry and the record as a whole (9.2.5) all refuse a crossing they cannot make value-preserving, and a deployed stack got every one of them wrong in the same way — by silently narrowing (as u16 on the identifier, a dropped target half, a right-shifted entry). Read together they say one thing: a crossing either preserves every value or does not happen. Vectors XV2–XV4 are the four refusals.

Note 1 to entry: 9.2.3 is an anti-stripping rule, and the reasoning deserves stating. The tag is computed over tier-native field widths, so re-encoding invalidates it. A crossing that re-signed would have to hold the key — making every tier bridge a member of every group whose traffic it carries. A crossing that stripped the tag would convert authenticated traffic to unauthenticated silently, downgrading exactly the frames someone bothered to sign. Refusing the crossing is the only option that neither spreads keys nor strips protection — the cost is that signed traffic is planned end-to-end within one tier, and that cost is accepted and visible.

10 End-to-end integrity

10.1 What the tag is

10.1.1 Where the integrity flag is set, the frame shall end with an HMAC-SHA256 tag: the first 8 bytes of the output at the compact tier, the full 32 at the extended tier.

10.1.2 The tag shall be computed by the origin and verified by the destination. A relay shall forward it unchanged and shall not be expected to verify it.

10.1.3 Verification shall compare in constant time, and a frame failing verification shall be discarded.

Note 1 to entry: "Failing verification" means failing under every key the receiver is entitled to try, and which keys those are is Layer 5's (10.3). A receiver holding an entanglement tries its keys after its own group's key fails (Layer 5, 7.1.2 c) — so discarding at the first failure would silently deliver no crossings at all, while looking correct to anyone reading this clause alone. (Added 2026-08-02: an implementer reading Layer 4 in isolation reached the wrong reading.)

10.1.4 A frame bearing an integrity tag shall carry a route record with at least one entry — the span (10.2.1) requires the origin, and a record of count zero has no entry zero to authenticate. A frame of a type listed in 8.1 with the integrity flag set and no route record, or a route record of count zero, shall be rejected as malformed. A GROUP_MGMT frame shall not carry an integrity tag: its authenticity is established by the means Layer 5 specifies.

Note 1 to entry: Before this clause, the integrity flag and the route flag were formally independent, leaving the span of a tagged route-less frame undefined. (Ruled 2026-08-01: the tag requires the route record it authenticates entry zero of, and GROUP_MGMT — the one type that carries no route record — settles its authenticity above this layer.)

Note 2 to entry: The forward reference in 10.1.4 is currently unsatisfied, and it is recorded here rather than left for a reader to discover. Layer 5 names no means: the type does not appear in that document at all. Note 2 to 8.3 forwards a second property — the frame's origin — and Layer 5, 7.2.2 states in terms that passing its gate does NOT establish which member sent a frame. So one forward names a means that does not exist and the other names a property that layer declines to give. Until it is answered, a receiver has no stated obligation before acting on an untagged GROUP_MGMT frame, and Layer 5, 10.1.2 permits such a frame to be delivered as unauthenticated. (Added 2026-08-04, PROVISIONAL(standard), finding SS274, on core-lane measurement; the gap is Layer 5's to close and is stated in its 13.11.)

10.2 The authenticated span

10.2.1 The tag shall be computed over, in order: the type value as one zero-padded byte; the message identifier at tier-native width; the origin (route record entry zero) at tier-native width; the event identifier hash; the target; the payload.

10.2.2 The hop limit, the replication budget, and every route record entry after the first shall not be covered.

10.2.3 The version and flag bits of byte 0 shall not be covered; the type bits shall be covered as 10.2.1 states.

Note 1 to entry: The span is chosen by one rule: a field is covered if and only if no relay ever changes it. The hop limit and budget are what relays exist to decrement; the route tail grows at every hop; everything else is fixed at origination. A span drawn anywhere else forces relays to re-sign, which they cannot, or leaves immutable fields forgeable, which 10.2 Note 2 shows is not hypothetical.

Note 2 to entry: Both halves of the duplicate-suppression key are inside the span, and each earned its place by an attack. With the message identifier outside it, an attacker rewrites the identifier on a valid frame — the tag still verifies — and the frame passes the duplicate cache again as new: a replay needing no key. With the origin outside it, the same attack via the other half of the key. Covering both makes the key unforgeable by a non-member — which is the whole of what covering it buys. Against a member it buys nothing: the tag is computed under the group key, so any member can produce a valid tag over any origin, and Layer 5, 7.2.2 states that passage establishes which group and never which member. Layer 3's keying on (origin, message identifier) is therefore sound against outsiders and forgeable within the group; Layer 3, 5.3.4 and 10.5 carry the consequence. (Scope corrected 2026-08-02, PROVISIONAL(supervisor). The note previously said the key was "safe to rely on" without naming against whom — a claim wider than its evidence, reached by naming the two attacks the span defeats and then generalising past them. Found by an implementer asking whether attribution inside a group is authenticated at all.)

Note 2a to entry: 10.2.1 defines the span; it does not say who computes it, and on a conforming implementation that is not the party that verifies the tag. Layer 0, 6.1 keeps trust material out of Layer 1–4 code, so the span is computed here and the tag is verified at Layer 5 — an agreement across a boundary this standard requires. Layer 5, 7.1.3a states what that seam must carry, because a verifier fed a byte sequence has no way to tell it is not the span, and a short span yields a tag that verifies while protecting nothing it omitted.

Note 3 to entry: A replayed frame — identical bytes, after the duplicate record expires — still delivers. That is accepted at this layer because events are idempotent by design. What a replay is never permitted to do is refresh anyone's liveness: presence is established by freshness evidence above this layer, and a captured frame replayed on schedule does not keep a dead hive alive.

10.3 Keys are not this layer's

10.3.1 The keys the tag is computed with, their derivation, and the decision of which key a receiver tries, shall be specified at Layer 5.

10.3.2 A frame may verify under the receiver's own group key or under a key shared between groups. Nothing in the frame states which; the receiver shall establish it by attempting verification, own-group first.

Note 1 to entry: There is no bit saying which key signed the frame because there are no bits left in byte 0 — and because a receiver holding several candidate keys can try them, whereas a wire flag claiming a key would still have to be checked by trying it. The flag would add a byte and remove nothing.

11 Payloads

11.1 An ordinary payload shall be CBOR, definite-length only.

11.2 An implementation shall accept and generate: unsigned and negative integers, byte strings, text strings, arrays, maps, false, true, null, and IEEE 754 floats.

11.3 A payload may be empty.

11.4 A receiver shall ignore map keys it does not recognise, and shall not treat their presence as an error.

Note 1 to entry: 11.4 is the extension model. New fields are added by adding keys, old receivers skip them, and nothing is versioned — which works only as long as 11.4 is honoured universally, and is why it is a requirement on receivers rather than advice to senders.

12 Conformance

Every requirement of this document is demonstrated by conformance evidence of one of the three classes of 01-terminology.md, 3.10 — behavioural, by construction, or by declaration. A requirement whose evidence is by construction is not thereby optional: the demonstration is an inspection a third party can perform without the implementer's cooperation, and its absence is non-conformance exactly as a failed behavioural test is. Behavioural evidence for any requirement of this document carries the control 01-terminology.md 4.2 to 4.5 requires — the same procedure run against a case the requirement excludes, giving a distinguishable non-satisfying result attributable to the requirement under test; a demonstration that could not have come out any other way is not evidence. (Added 2026-08-02, PROVISIONAL(supervisor); the control sentence added the same day, finding SS183.)

12.1 An implementation conforms to this document where it:

a) encodes and parses both tiers exactly as Clause 4 lays them out;

b) generates only the types of Clause 5 and handles each as stated;

c) hashes names exactly as Clause 7 states, normalisation included;

d) maintains the route record as Clause 8 requires, including dropping origin-less frames of the types that require one;

e) parses at the bearer-declared tier, never mixes ordinary tiers on a bearer, and refuses the crossings 9.2 refuses;

f) computes, forwards and verifies the integrity tag exactly as Clause 10 states, over exactly that span; and

g) encodes payloads as Clause 11 states and ignores unknown keys.

12.2 Conformance shall be demonstrated against byte-exact test vectors covering: each tier's layout; each message type; frames with and without route record and tag; the route record at zero, one and full count, demonstrating an unambiguous payload boundary at the compact tier; the authenticated span, including a vector that fails verification for each covered field mutated and a vector pair per field outside the span demonstrating the tag byte-identical under that field's mutation; and the normalisation cases of 7.1.2.

Note 0 to entry: The second half of the span requirement is the one over-coverage makes necessary: an implementation that authenticates a mutable field — the hop limit, say — passes every required-to-fail vector and cannot relay, because the first decrement invalidates its own tag. Only the byte-identical pair on each uncovered field catches the span drawn too wide; the required-to-fail vectors catch it drawn too narrow.

12.3 The vectors shall be part of this standard. They do not yet exist — 14.4 records the debt, and until they land, conformance to this document cannot be demonstrated as 12.2 requires.

Note 1 to entry: 12.2's mutation vectors are the ones that matter most and the ones most often omitted. A vector that verifies proves an implementation can agree with itself; a vector required to fail proves the span is drawn where this document says it is. An implementation passing only the positive vectors may be signing the wrong bytes and will interoperate with itself indefinitely.

Note 2 to entry: 12.3 is the reason prose disagreements about this layer are settleable. Where the text and a vector disagree, one of them is wrong in public, and the disagreement is a defect report rather than two implementations.

13 Security considerations

13.1 Everything in a frame except the payload's meaning is visible to every hive within radio range, member or not. The wire format hides nothing and is not designed to.

13.2 The integrity tag authenticates the span to holders of the key. It provides no confidentiality — an unencrypted payload under a valid tag is authentic and public. Encryption of payloads is established above this layer.

13.3 An unsigned frame is exactly as trustworthy as its transport, which is to say not at all. Nothing at this layer distinguishes a forged unsigned frame from a genuine one, and every protection against acting on forgeries lives above the trust boundary.

13.4 The truncated compact tag trades security margin for airtime deliberately. 64 bits against forgery, with a short duplicate window and idempotent effects behind it, is the accepted position; deployments needing more use the extended tier end-to-end.

14 What is not decided at this layer

This clause states gaps and contains no requirements.

14.1 A hive bridging bearers of both tiers. 9.2 states what happens to a frame at a tier boundary, and today no specified platform carries both tiers at once. A hive that did would need the crossing rules of 9.2 applied inside one device, and how its two sides share one duplicate cache and one neighbour table is unspecified.

14.2 Anti-replay for non-idempotent traffic — settled above this layer (2026-08-01). 10.2 Note 3 accepts replay of identical frames on the grounds that events are idempotent. The traffic that is not idempotent is exactly the traffic requiring member-attributable evidence, and that evidence now carries its own freshness token (Layer 5, 7.4.3) — the resolution landed as evidence content, not as a frame field, so this layer's format is unchanged. (Settled by ruling.)

14.3 The two reserved type codes and the reserved type 1. Three of eight type values are unassigned. The assignment authority is this document — an implementation that invents a type has forked the protocol.

14.4 The vector set. 12.2 and 12.3 require byte-exact vectors as part of this standard, and none exist yet. Until they land, two implementations can each believe themselves conformant with no way to prove it to each other — the debt is recorded on the register, and producing the set is formats-companion work.
